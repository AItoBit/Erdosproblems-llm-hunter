\subsection*{1) OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE AUDIT}
For $a_n=(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log n$, the problem asks that the empirical
distribution converge to a continuous distribution. The source supplies
a sieve-based small-gap upper bound and correctly leaves convergence open.
Its assertion that there are no prime pairs of odd difference is false:
$2$ and $2+h$ can both be prime. There is at most one such pair for each odd
$h$. Consecutive gaps for $n\ge2$ are even, so restricting that argument to
even $h$ removes the error without changing the intended estimate.

\subsection*{2) WORK: WHAT COMPACTNESS DOES AND DOES NOT GIVE}
Assume the mean estimate already used in the source,
\[
 \frac1{N-1}\sum_{n=2}^N a_n\longrightarrow1.
\]
Define probability measures $\mu_N=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{n=2}^N\delta_{a_n}$.
For every $R>0$, Markov's inequality gives
$\mu_N([R,\infty))\le(1+o(1))/R$. These measures are tight.
One can prove subsequential compactness directly: choose successively
subsequences on which $\mu_N([0,q])$ converges for each nonnegative rational
$q$, use a diagonal subsequence, then take right limits to obtain a
nondecreasing right-continuous distribution function. Tightness gives
total mass one; squeezing between rational endpoints gives convergence
at every continuity point of the limiting function.

Every resulting probability measure $\mu$ satisfies
\[
 \int_0^\infty t\,d\mu(t)\le1.
\]
For proof, apply weak convergence to $\min(t,R)$, whose average is at most
$1+o(1)$, and let $R$ increase. Equality of the limiting mean is not justified
without uniform integrability: a small amount of mass can escape to large
values while preserving total first moment.

If one additionally retains the source's small-gap estimate
$\limsup_N\mu_N([0,c))\le Cc$, then the open-set inequality for weak
convergence gives $\mu([0,c))\le Cc$. Letting $c$ decrease to zero shows
$\mu(\{0\})=0$. This last conclusion uses the stated sieve input; the
compactness and moment arguments above do not require it.

\subsection*{3) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
Neither tightness nor the absence of an atom at zero implies uniqueness of
the subsequential measure. They also do not exclude an atom at a positive
point. For example, $\delta_1$ and $(\delta_{1/2}+\delta_{3/2})/2$ both have
mean one and no atom at zero. This is an illustration of the logical gap,
not a model of the actual prime gaps.
