\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem 171: density Hales--Jewett}

\subsection{1) Statement and scope}
For each $t\geq1$ and $\epsilon>0$, must every
$A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t\}^N$ of size at least $\epsilon t^N$ contain
a combinatorial line when $N$ is sufficiently large?
A line has a nonempty set $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ of variable
coordinates: its $i$th word has symbol $i$ in every coordinate of $S$
and a fixed common symbol at each remaining coordinate.

The general assertion is the established density Hales--Jewett theorem
of Furstenberg and Katznelson, with a later combinatorial proof by the
Polymath project. This attempt gives a complete elementary proof for
$t\leq2$. It does not reconstruct the theorem for all alphabets.

\subsection{2) Two symbols reduce to an antichain problem}
Identify a word in $\{1,2\}^N$ with the set of positions containing $2$.
Two different words form a combinatorial line precisely when their
associated sets $B,C$ satisfy $B\subsetneq C$: the variable coordinates
are $C\setminus B$, with the other coordinates fixed.
Consequently a family without a line is an antichain of subsets of $[N]$.

Choose a permutation of $[N]$ uniformly at random, and consider the chain
consisting of its initial sets of lengths $0,1,\ldots,N$.
For a fixed $k$-set $B$, the probability that this chain contains $B$
is $1/\binom Nk$, since exactly $k!(N-k)!$ of the $N!$ permutations
put its members first. A chain meets an antichain $\mathcal A$ at most
once, so taking expectations gives
\[
 \sum_{B\in\mathcal A}\frac1{\binom N{|B|}}\leq1.
\]
Every binomial coefficient is at most $\binom N{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}$.
Therefore
\[
 |\mathcal A|\leq\binom N{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}.
\]
The middle layer attains this bound. Stirling's formula now gives
\[
 2^{-N}\binom N{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
 \sim\sqrt{\frac2{\pi N}}\longrightarrow0.
\]
For fixed $\epsilon>0$ this is smaller than $\epsilon$ for all large
$N$. Thus any family of density at least $\epsilon$ has a line when
$t=2$. This proof also gives a threshold of order $\epsilon^{-2}$,
up to an absolute constant. For $t=1$ and $N\geq1$, the unique word
itself is a line under the stated definition, so the assertion is immediate.

\subsection{3) The missing general step}
For $t\geq3$, an inclusion chain of sets no longer encodes all $t$
words of a combinatorial line. The preceding antichain argument does
not yield a density bound tending to zero for these alphabets.
One needs the general density-increment or ergodic argument; none is
provided here. The published theorem resolves the original problem,
but citing its conclusion would not extend the proof just given.
