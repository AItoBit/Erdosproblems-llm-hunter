\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem \#441: sets with pairwise bounded least common multiples}
\noindent Partial reconstruction and an exact small counterexample, 24 September 2026.

\subsection*{1) Statement}
Let $g(N)$ be the largest size of $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ such that $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\le N$ for every $a,b\in A$. Determine $g(N)$. Is it attained by
\[B_N=\{a\in\mathbb N:a\le\sqrt{N/2}\}
\ \cup\ \{a\in2\mathbb N:\sqrt{N/2}\le a\le\sqrt{2N}\}?\]
We give a valid construction, a universal upper bound of the correct order, and a counterexample to the literal assertion that this construction is always optimal. The sharp asymptotic and arbitrarily large counterexamples are not proved here.

\subsection*{2) Verify the proposed construction}
Put $t=\sqrt{N/2}$, so that $\sqrt{2N}=2t$. Two elements of the first part have product at most $t^2=N/2$. One element from each part has product at most $2t^2=N$. Two elements of the second part are even, and therefore
\[\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\frac{ab}{\gcd(a,b)}\le\frac{(2t)^2}{2}=N.\]
The same conclusions hold for equal elements; alternatively their least common multiple is just the element itself. Every listed element is at most $N$ (the case $N=1$ is immediate). Thus $B_N$ is admissible. Counting integers and even integers, with a possible shared endpoint counted once, gives
\[|B_N|=t+\frac t2+O(1)=\frac3{2\sqrt2}\sqrt N+O(1).\tag{1}\]

\subsection*{3) A telescoping universal upper bound}
Order any admissible set increasingly. There are at most $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ elements at most $\sqrt N$. Let its remaining elements be $b_1<\cdots<b_r$. If $r\ge2$, each adjacent pair is admissible, and
\[\frac1N\le\frac{\gcd(b_j,b_{j+1})}{b_jb_{j+1}}
\le\frac{b_{j+1}-b_j}{b_jb_{j+1}}
=\frac1{b_j}-\frac1{b_{j+1}}.\]
Summing gives $(r-1)/N<1/b_1<1/\sqrt N$. For $r\le1$ the resulting bound is immediate as well. Hence
\[g(N)\le2\sqrt N+1.\tag{2}\]
Together, (1) and (2) prove the square-root order of magnitude with explicit constants.

\subsection*{4) An exact counterexample at $N=4$}
The proposed set is $B_4=\{1,2\}$, whereas $\{1,2,4\}$ is admissible and has size three. Moreover $g(4)=3$: an admissible set containing $3$ can contain neither $2$ nor $4$, because their least common multiples with $3$ are $6$ and $12$. Such a set has size at most two. A set omitting $3$ has at most three elements, and the displayed example attains three.

Thus the proposed construction is not optimal for every $N$. This small example does not establish the published, stronger statement that its deficit is unbounded along infinitely many $N$.
