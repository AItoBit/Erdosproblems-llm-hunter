\subsection{1) Statement and current negative resolution}
For every function $f(n)\to\infty$, is there a graph of infinite
chromatic number such that each $n$-vertex subgraph becomes bipartite
after deletion of at most $f(n)$ edges?

\subsection{2) A bounded certificate for short odd cycles}
Fix an integer $L\geq2$ and put $B_0=0$ and
$B_k=L+L^2+\cdots+L^k$. Call a family of odd cycles of length at
most $L$ \emph{$k$-rich} if every set of fewer than $k$ edges fails
to meet some member of the family.

We prove by induction that every $k$-rich family has a $k$-rich
subfamily whose union has at most $B_k$ edges. At $k=0$ choose the
empty family; the defining assertion is vacuous. Suppose the result
holds for $k$ and let $\mathcal S$ be $(k+1)$-rich. It is nonempty,
so choose $C\in\mathcal S$. For $e\in E(C)$, let
$\mathcal S_e$ consist of those cycles avoiding $e$.
If some set $F$ of fewer than $k$ edges hit every member of
$\mathcal S_e$, then $F\cup\{e\}$, of size at most $k$, would hit
every member of $\mathcal S$. This is impossible. Hence each
$\mathcal S_e$ is $k$-rich and has a suitable subfamily
$\mathcal S'_e$ on at most $B_k$ edges.

Take $\mathcal S'=\{C\}\cup\bigcup_{e\in E(C)}\mathcal S'_e$.
Its edge union has size at most $L+LB_k=B_{k+1}$. Given $|F|<k+1$,
either $F$ misses $C$, or choose $e\in F\cap E(C)$. In the latter
case $|F\setminus\{e\}|<k$, so some cycle of $\mathcal S'_e$
misses $F\setminus\{e\}$. It also misses $e$ and hence misses $F$.
This proves the induction, including for an infinite original family.

Consequently, if every subgraph with at most $B_{d+1}$ edges can be
made bipartite by deleting at most $d$ edges, then all odd cycles
of length at most $L$ in the entire graph can be hit with at most
$d$ edges. Otherwise the family would be $(d+1)$-rich; its finite
certificate would contradict the assumed bipartization bound.

\subsection{3) Checking the four-layer coloring join}
Suppose vertex layers have integer indices and every edge changes
the index by at most one. Let $a$ and $b$ be proper colorings,
defined on overlapping inner and outer regions, and suppose both
use only colors $1,2$ on layers $t,t+1,t+2,t+3$.
Start with $a$ on layers at most $t+1$ and $b$ on layers at least
$t+2$. On the middle two layers recolor to $3$ precisely those
vertices $v$ satisfying $a(v)=2$ and $b(v)=1$.

The recolored vertices are independent: an edge between two of them
would have both endpoints of color $2$ under the proper coloring
$a$. At the inner boundary, a recolored vertex meets only colors
$1,2$ in layer $t$; the same holds at the outer boundary with layer
$t+3$. Edges within an unchanged-color region remain proper.
The only possible original conflict therefore crossed from $u$
in layer $t+1$ to $v$ in layer $t+2$ with $a(u)=b(v)$.
If this common value is $1$, properness of $a$ gives $a(v)=2$,
so $v$ is recolored. If it is $2$, properness of $b$ gives
$b(u)=1$, so $u$ is recolored. Exactly one endpoint is recolored
in either case. Every edge is therefore properly colored.

\subsection{4) Parameter issue and remaining gap}
The original hypothesis counts vertices; the certificate counts
edges. A graph with at most $m$ edges has at most $2m$ nonisolated
vertices, so for a nondecreasing $f$ the vertex hypothesis gives
an edge bound $D(m)\leq f(2m)$. Isolated vertices do not affect
bipartiteness. This factor cannot simply be ignored in a quantitative
argument. Restricting to a carefully chosen nondecreasing divergent
$f$ is legitimate for a disproof of the universal assertion.

What is still missing from this attempt is a fully specified sequence
of cycle-length cutoffs, deletion budgets and neighborhood radii
which always supplies the required four-layer overlaps and preserves
the coloring induction. The certificate and the join do not alone
establish that sequence. The source's stated disproof is accepted as
context; this note does not claim a complete independent disproof.
