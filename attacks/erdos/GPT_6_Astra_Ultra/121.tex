\section{FORMAL RESTATEMENT}
\textbf{Erd\H{o}s \#121; partial reconstruction, 2026-09-23.}
Let $F_k(N)$ be the maximum size of $A\subseteq[N]$ for which no $k$
distinct elements have square product. Is $F_5(N)=(1-o(1))N$, and more
generally does this hold for odd $k\ge5$?

\section{QUICK LITERATURE AND CONTEXT CHECK}
The assertion is known to be false: Tao's primary paper proves
$F_k(N)\le(1-c_k)N$ for sufficiently large $N$ and fixed $k\ge4$, with
$c_k>0$. We prove elementary comparison results and the precise
probabilistic transfer step, while isolating the analytic construction
needed for the full negative answer. The latter is cited, not claimed
as independently proved here. No novelty is claimed.

\section{WORK}
\subsection*{The two-element case}
Write uniquely $n=s(n)t^2$ with $s(n)$ squarefree. Then $uv$ is a
square if and only if $s(u)=s(v)$. An admissible set for $F_2$ contains
at most one number per kernel, and the squarefree numbers themselves
achieve this bound. Thus
\[
 F_2(N)=\sum_{n\le N}\mu(n)^2
 =\sum_{d\le\sqrt N}\mu(d)\lfloor N/d^2\rfloor
 =\frac{6}{\pi^2}N+O(\sqrt N).
\]
The final equality uses the absolutely convergent Euler product
$\sum_d\mu(d)/d^2=1/\zeta(2)$; its tail and the floor errors are each
$O(\sqrt N)$.

\subsection*{A large set avoiding every odd square product}
Let $A_N$ consist of integers at most $N$ with a prime divisor
$p>\sqrt N$. Each has exactly one such prime, appearing to exponent
one. In a product of an odd number of members, the total number of
these large-prime occurrences is odd, so at least one prime occurs an
odd number of times. The product cannot be a square, whether or not
the selected elements are distinct. Consequently
\[
 F_k(N)\ge |A_N|\quad\hbox{for every odd }k.
\]
Distinct primes above $\sqrt N$ cannot divide the same integer at most
$N$, so, by the prime number theorem and Mertens' prime reciprocal sum,
\[
 |A_N|=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\lfloor N/p\rfloor
 =N\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac1p+O(\pi(N))
 =(\log2+o(1))N.                                         \tag{1}
\]
This is a genuine positive-density lower construction, but does not
approach density one.

\subsection*{A transfer lemma isolating the upper-bound difficulty}
Suppose a random tuple $(X_1,\ldots,X_k)$ has square product always,
and there is an event $E$ such that $X_i\in[N]$ on $E$ and
\[
 \Pr(E)\ge aL^{-k},\qquad
 \Pr(E\cap\{X_i=x\})\le b/(NL^k),\qquad
 \Pr(\exists i<j:X_i=X_j)=o(L^{-k}),                       \tag{2}
\]
where $L=\log N$ and $a,b>0$ depend only on $k$.
If $A\subseteq[N]$ omits at most $aN/(4kb)$ elements, the union bound
shows that the probability of $E$ together with some $X_i\notin A$
is at most $a/(4L^k)$. For sufficiently large $N$, the collision
probability is also at most $a/(4L^k)$. Thus with positive probability
all the $X_i$ are distinct members of $A$ and have square product.
This proves
\[
 F_k(N)\le (1-a/(4kb))N                                  \tag{3}
\]
under (2).

The algebraic source of such tuples is particularly simple. Give the
edges of a complete graph on $k$ vertices independent positive integer
labels $D_{ij}=D_{ji}$ and put $X_i=\prod_{j\ne i}D_{ij}$.
Then $\prod_iX_i=\prod_{i<j}D_{ij}^2$ automatically. What is difficult
is choosing the distributions so the tuple has the uniform marginal
bounds in (2) after conditioning on its size. Uniform edge labels
alone do not establish those bounds.
Tao's Proposition 2.1 constructs precisely (2), using small squarefree
factors, larger prime factors, and prime-distribution estimates. Thus
(3), together with that external proposition, reproduces the known
negative answer. Its uniform probability estimate has not been proved
in this attempt.

\section{VERIFICATION}
All products in the question require distinct elements; the transfer
lemma explicitly removes collisions. The lower construction (1) works
simultaneously for every odd length, whereas the upper-bound constants
are for fixed $k$. Small exact subset searches in the batch checks
confirm the squarefree-kernel rule and the odd-product construction.
They do not verify Tao's asymptotic probabilistic estimates.

\section{FINAL STATUS}
\textbf{UNRESOLVED within this partial reconstruction; the problem is
already disproved in the literature.}
The elementary bounds and transfer implication are proved. Completing
an independent reconstruction requires the distributional estimates
in (2), particularly uniformity in $x$; simply restating Proposition 2.1
is not counted as proving that missing part.
