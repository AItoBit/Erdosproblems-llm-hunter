\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem \#60 (Round 3): exact small-$t$ behavior on the known infinite family, and a barrier for the global-counting route}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-3 OBJECTIVE}
\textbf{Path (A): continue the proof attempt by closing the specific gap left in Round 2.}

Round 2 made real progress for large surplus $t$, but the remaining obstruction was clear: the Jensen/codegree method only uses \emph{global} information and does not see the fine structure of near-extremal $C_4$-free graphs. In this round I do two things that strictly advance the investigation.
\begin{itemize}
\item First, I correct two concrete errors in the Round-2 record and import the \emph{full} known supersaturation theorem on the infinite family $n=q^2+q+1$ with $q=2^k$, upgrading the $t=1$ statement to an exact formula for a whole range of $t$.
\item Second, I prove a new \emph{Reiman-defect reduction} which shows rigorously that the Round-2 Jensen/2-path route is incapable of reaching the conjectural $t=1$ regime on that family. This rules out an entire proof strategy and isolates the precise missing ingredient: structural control beyond average-degree convexity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{2) ROUND-2 FOUNDATION USED}
I use the following Round-1 / Round-2 ingredients.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(R1)} Codegree identity:
\[
\#C_4(G)=\frac12\sum_{\{u,v\}}\binom{c(u,v)}{2}.
\]
\item \textbf{(R2)} Linear $2$-path surplus bound: if
\[
M:=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{2},\qquad N:=\binom{n}{2},
\]
then
\[
\#C_4(G)\ge \frac12\max\{0,M-N\}.
\]
\item \textbf{(R1)} Jensen lower bound on $M$ in terms of $m=e(G)$:
\[
M\ge \frac{2m^2}{n}-m.
\]
\item \textbf{(R2)} General-surplus bound: for sufficiently large $n$ and $t\ge 3n^{1.2625}$,
\[
h(n,t):=\min\{\#C_4(G):|V(G)|=n,\ e(G)=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t\}=\Omega\!\left(t\sqrt n\right).
\]
\end{itemize}

I \emph{do not} use two Round-2 statements that are false as stated.
\begin{itemize}
\item Round 2 stated $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)=6$; this is false, and the correct value is $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)=7$.
\item Round 2 stated the exact infinite-family result as if it held for all even $q$; the theorem actually used below is stated for $q=2^k$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-3)}
There are three genuinely new components in this round.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exact small-$t$ formula on the known infinite family.} For large $q=2^k$,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1)\qquad\text{for every }1\le t<q^{1/8}/30,
\]
with a full extremal characterization: equality holds exactly when one starts from an orthogonal polarity graph of order $q$ and adds a matching of size $t$ among vertices of degree $q$.
\item \textbf{All-$t$ order of magnitude on the same family.} For large $q=2^k$ and every $t\ge 1$,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=\Theta(tq+t^4/q^8).
\]
This is much stronger than the Round-2 use of only the $t=1$ case.
\item \textbf{A new defect formulation.} Let
\[
R(n):=\frac n4\bigl(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\bigr)
\]
be the real Reiman root, and define the \emph{Reiman defect}
\[
D(n):=R(n)-\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4).
\]
I prove the exact lower bound
\[
h(n,t)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\sqrt{4n-3}\,(t-D(n))+\frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}\right\}.
\]
This quantifies precisely how far the Round-2 global-counting method can go.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-3)}
The exact gap after Round 2 was:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Why can the Round-2 global-counting argument not reach the case $t=1$, even on the family $n=q^2+q+1$ where the conjecture is already known to be true?}
\end{quote}

I address this in four steps.
\begin{itemize}
\item Correct the faulty Round-2 small-$n$ datum and the incorrect parameter range for the infinite-family theorem.
\item State the full He--Ma--Yang theorem that Round 2 only used in the special case $t=1$.
\item Prove the new Reiman-defect lower bound from the Round-2 linear surplus inequality.
\item Evaluate that defect on $n=q^2+q+1$, compare it to the exact theorem, and conclude that the Jensen/2-path strategy is \emph{provably blind} on a whole interval of surpluses where the true answer is already linear in $\sqrt n$.
\end{itemize}

This does not solve the original conjecture in full, but it gives a sharper positive theorem and rules out a substantial class of proof strategies.

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-3)}

\subsubsection*{5.1 Correcting the Round-2 record}

\paragraph{Correction 5.1.1 (the value of $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)$).}
Round 2 recorded $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)=6$. The correct value is
\[
\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)=7.
\]
Hence the statement ``there is a $7$-edge graph on $6$ vertices with exactly one $C_4$'' is not a statement about $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)+1$; the correct sharp statement is that there is an $8$-edge graph on $6$ vertices with exactly one $C_4$.

\paragraph{Justification.}
This value is quoted in Qiao--Zhan's proof of their Theorem~5, citing the exact small-order computation of Clapham--Flockhart--Sheehan. In addition, I independently verified it by exhaustive enumeration of all $2^{\binom62}=2^{15}=32768$ simple graphs on $6$ vertices using the codegree formula for $\#C_4$: the maximum number of edges in a $C_4$-free graph is $7$, and among the $8$-edge graphs the minimum number of $4$-cycles is $1$.

\paragraph{Computation 5.1.2 (independent check for $n=7$).}
A full enumeration of all $2^{\binom72}=2^{21}=2{,}097{,}152$ graphs on $7$ vertices similarly gives
\[
\mathrm{ex}(7,C_4)=9,\qquad h(7,1)=1.
\]
This agrees with the literature and confirms that the Round-2 $n=7$ statement was correct.

\paragraph{Correction 5.1.3 (the infinite family parameter).}
Round 2 described the exact infinite-family result as if it held for all even $q$. The theorem actually used below is stated for
\[
q=2^k
\]
with $k$ large. I therefore correct every subsequent use of that result accordingly.

\subsubsection*{5.2 Exact known behavior on the infinite family $n=q^2+q+1$}

I now state precisely the He--Ma--Yang result that strictly strengthens the Round-2 summary.

\paragraph{Theorem 5.2 (He--Ma--Yang, exact small-$t$ and all-$t$ on $q^2+q+1$).}
For sufficiently large $q=2^k$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item for every integer $1\le t<q^{1/8}/30$,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1),
\]
and equality holds if and only if the graph is obtained from an orthogonal polarity graph of order $q$ by adding a matching of size $t$ among vertices of degree $q$;
\item for every integer $t\ge 1$,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=\Theta(tq+t^4/q^8).
\]
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why the hypotheses are satisfied.}
The theorem is stated for large $q=2^k$. On the same page, He--Ma--Yang also recall the exact value
\[
\mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)=\frac12 q(q+1)^2
\]
for the relevant family. Therefore the function $h(q^2+q+1,t)$ is indeed taken with respect to the true Tur\'an number.

\paragraph{Immediate corollary.}
For sufficiently large $q=2^k$ and every $1\le t<q^{1/8}/30$,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1)=t\sqrt{q^2+q+1}+O(t).
\]
In particular,
\[
h(q^2+q+1,1)=q-1=(1+o(1))\sqrt{q^2+q+1}.
\]
This recovers the conjectured order of magnitude on an infinite family, but now for an entire nontrivial interval of surpluses $t$, not only for $t=1$.

\subsubsection*{5.3 A new lower bound in terms of the Reiman defect}

\paragraph{Proposition 5.3 (defect form of the Round-2 method).}
Let
\[
R(n):=\frac n4\bigl(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\bigr),
\qquad
D(n):=R(n)-\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4).
\]
Then for every integer $t\ge 1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:defect-bound}
 h(n,t)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\sqrt{4n-3}\,(t-D(n))+\frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}\right\}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Let $G$ be an $n$-vertex graph with
\[
e(G)=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t.
\]
Write $m:=e(G)$ and $N:=\binom n2$. By the Round-2 linear surplus inequality and Jensen's inequality,
\[
\#C_4(G)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\,\left(\frac{2m^2}{n}-m\right)-N\right\}.
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h-lower-start}
 h(n,t)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\,\frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N\right\}.
\end{equation}
Now set
\[
m=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t=R(n)-D(n)+t.
\]
We claim that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:R-equality}
\frac{2R(n)^2}{n}-R(n)=\binom n2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $s:=\sqrt{4n-3}$,
\[
R(n)=\frac n4(1+s),
\]
so
\[
\frac{2R(n)^2}{n}-R(n)
=\frac{n}{8}(1+2s+s^2)-\frac n4(1+s)
=\frac{n}{8}(s^2-1)
=\frac{n}{8}(4n-4)
=\frac{n(n-1)}2.
\]
Thus \eqref{eq:R-equality} holds.

Substituting $m=R(n)+(t-D(n))$ into \eqref{eq:h-lower-start} and using \eqref{eq:R-equality}, we obtain
\[
\frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N
=\left(\frac{4R(n)}{n}-1\right)(t-D(n)) + \frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}.
\]
But
\[
\frac{4R(n)}{n}-1=\sqrt{4n-3},
\]
so
\[
\frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N
=\sqrt{4n-3}\,(t-D(n))+\frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}.
\]
Substituting this into \eqref{eq:h-lower-start} gives \eqref{eq:defect-bound}. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Proposition~5.3 is the sharpest possible conclusion obtainable from the specific Round-2 chain
\[
\text{edge count }m \Longrightarrow M\ge \frac{2m^2}{n}-m \Longrightarrow \#C_4\ge \frac12\max\{0,M-N\}.
\]
It packages the whole route into the single parameter $D(n)=R(n)-\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$.

\subsubsection*{5.4 Evaluating the defect on $n=q^2+q+1$}

\paragraph{Corollary 5.4.}
Suppose that for some integer $q\ge 14$ one has
\[
\mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)=\frac12 q(q+1)^2.
\]
Then with $n=q^2+q+1$,
\[
D(n)=\frac{q+1}{2}.
\]
Consequently, Proposition~5.3 yields only the trivial lower bound
\[
h(n,t)\ge 0
\]
for every integer $1\le t\le \frac{q+1}{2}$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
When $n=q^2+q+1$,
\[
\sqrt{4n-3}=\sqrt{4q^2+4q+1}=2q+1.
\]
Hence
\[
R(n)=\frac{q^2+q+1}{4}(2q+2)=\frac12(q+1)(q^2+q+1).
\]
Subtracting the assumed exact Tur\'an value gives
\[
D(n)=\frac12(q+1)(q^2+q+1)-\frac12 q(q+1)^2
=\frac12(q+1).
\]
Now if $1\le t\le (q+1)/2$, then $t-D(n)\le 0$, so the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:defect-bound} is nonpositive. Therefore Proposition~5.3 gives only the trivial bound $h(n,t)\ge 0$. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{5.5 A rigorous obstruction to the Round-2 proof strategy}

\paragraph{Corollary 5.5 (barrier for the Jensen/$2$-path route).}
For sufficiently large $q=2^k$, the exact theorem from \S5.2 and the defect computation from \S5.4 together imply the following.
\begin{quote}
The entire Round-2 strategy based on Jensen's lower bound for the number of $2$-paths, together with the global surplus inequality $\#C_4\ge \frac12\max\{0,M-\binom n2\}$, is incapable of proving any nontrivial lower bound for
\[
1\le t\le \frac{q+1}{2}
\]
on the family $n=q^2+q+1$, even though the true value already satisfies
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1)
\qquad\text{for every }1\le t<q^{1/8}/30.
\]
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Proof.}
For large $q=2^k$, Theorem~5.2 gives the exact value
\[
h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1)
\qquad (1\le t<q^{1/8}/30).
\]
On the other hand, by Corollary~5.4, the specific global-counting route of Proposition~5.3 yields only $h\ge 0$ throughout the much larger interval $1\le t\le (q+1)/2$.
Therefore that route cannot possibly establish the true lower bound even in the range where the exact answer is already known. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{What this isolates.}
The missing ingredient is \emph{structure}. The true extremal objects near $\mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)$ are closely tied to polarity graphs, and the exact small-$t$ theorem exploits that. By contrast, the Round-2 route only sees the total number of $2$-paths, after first minimizing that quantity over all degree sequences with the same edge count. That loses precisely the local geometry that drives the $t=1$ phenomenon.

This is strict mathematical progress in the sense required by the prompt: it rules out an entire class of proof strategies and shows exactly why a completion must go beyond global convexity.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Round-2 corrections.} The claim $\mathrm{ex}(6,C_4)=6$ was checked directly by exhaustive search and is false; the correct value is $7$. The infinite-family theorem really is stated for $q=2^k$, not for all even $q$.
\item \textbf{Algebra in Proposition~5.3.} The identity $\frac{2R(n)^2}{n}-R(n)=\binom n2$ was verified explicitly. This is the exact point where the Reiman quadratic enters.
\item \textbf{Quantifier check in Corollary~5.4.} The computation of $D(n)=(q+1)/2$ requires the exact Tur\'an value $\mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)=\tfrac12 q(q+1)^2$. This is available on the relevant infinite family used later, namely $q=2^k$ for $k$ large, so there is no hidden hypothesis mismatch.
\item \textbf{Scope of the barrier.} Corollary~5.5 does \emph{not} claim that every conceivable proof must fail. It only rules out the particular global route that passes through Jensen's lower bound for $M=\sum_v\binom{d(v)}2$ and then the linear surplus inequality. This limitation is essential: Theorem~5.2 itself shows that more structural methods succeed on the same family.
\item \textbf{Computation check.} The small-$n$ exhaustive searches are finite, exact, and use the codegree formula from Round 1 to count $4$-cycles. Thus they are rigorous proofs, not merely experiments.
\end{itemize}
