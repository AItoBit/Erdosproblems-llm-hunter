\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}
\textbf{Lemma (two-term criterion).}
Let \(0<u<v\) with \((u,v)=1\).
Then
\[
  \frac{u}{v}=\frac1x+\frac1y
\]
for some integers \(1<x<y\) if and only if there exists a divisor \(d\mid v^2\) such that
\[
  u\mid (v+d).
\]
In that case one may take
\[
  x=\frac{v+d}{u},
  \qquad
  y=\frac{v+v^2/d}{u}.
\]

\medskip
\textit{Proof.}
Starting from
\[
  \frac{u}{v}=\frac1x+\frac1y,
\]
clear denominators to get
\[
  uxy=v(x+y).
\]
Rearranging gives
\[
  (ux-v)(uy-v)=v^2.
\]
So if a two-term decomposition exists, then \(d:=ux-v\) is a divisor of \(v^2\), and clearly \(u\mid (v+d)\).
Conversely, assume \(d\mid v^2\) and \(u\mid (v+d)\), and set \(e:=v^2/d\).
Because \((u,v)=1\) and \(d\mid v^2\), we also have \((u,d)=1\).
From \(d\equiv -v\pmod u\) and \(de=v^2\) it follows that
\[
  e\equiv v^2 d^{-1}\equiv -v\pmod u.
\]
Hence both \((v+d)/u\) and \((v+e)/u\) are integers, and substituting them into
\[
  (ux-v)(uy-v)=v^2
\]
shows that they indeed give a two-term Egyptian decomposition.
\hfill \(\square\)

\medskip
\textbf{Theorem.}
Let \(p\ge 11\) be prime.
Then \((p-1)/p\) has no three-term Egyptian-fraction decomposition.
Equivalently,
\[
  N(p-1,p)\ge 4
  \qquad (p\ge 11\text{ prime}).
\]
Therefore the only primes with \(N(p-1,p)\le 3\) are
\[
  p=2,3,5,7.
\]

\medskip
\textit{Proof.}
Suppose for contradiction that
\[
  \frac{p-1}{p}=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z
\]
with integers \(1<x<y<z\).
If \(x\ge 3\), then
\[
  \frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z\le \frac13+\frac14+\frac15=\frac{47}{60},
\]
whereas for \(p\ge 11\),
\[
  \frac{p-1}{p}\ge \frac{10}{11}>\frac{47}{60}.
\]
So necessarily \(x=2\).
Subtracting \(1/2\) gives
\[
  \frac{p-2}{2p}=\frac1y+\frac1z.
\]
Now \(p\) is odd, so
\[
  (p-2,2p)=1.
\]
Apply the lemma with
\[
  u=p-2,
  \qquad
  v=2p.
\]
Then there must exist a divisor \(d\mid 4p^2\) such that
\[
  p-2\mid 2p+d.
\]
Every divisor of \(4p^2\) is of the form \(2^i p^j\) with \(0\le i,j\le 2\).
Modulo \(p-2\) we have \(p\equiv 2\), hence
\[
  d\equiv 2^{i+j}\pmod{p-2}.
\]
Therefore the divisibility condition implies
\[
  p-2\mid 4+2^{i+j}.
\]
But \(i+j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), so the right-hand side can only be one of
\[
  5,\ 6,\ 8,\ 12,\ 20.
\]
Since \(p\ge 11\) is odd prime, \(p-2\) is an odd integer at least \(9\), and no such integer divides any of \(5,6,8,12,20\).
Contradiction.
Hence no three-term decomposition exists.

For the small prime exceptions we have explicit decompositions
\[
  \frac12=\frac12,
  \qquad
  \frac23=\frac12+\frac16,
\]
\[
  \frac45=\frac12+\frac14+\frac1{20},
  \qquad
  \frac67=\frac12+\frac13+\frac1{42}.
\]
Thus the only primes with \(N(p-1,p)\le 3\) are \(2,3,5,7\).
\hfill \(\square\)

\medskip
\textbf{A concrete obstruction to the ``just study \((b-1)/b\)'' strategy.}
Using the exact iterative-deepening search from Round~1, I checked that
\[
  N(78,79)=5,
\]
for example via
\[
  \frac{78}{79}=\frac12+\frac13+\frac17+\frac1{90}+\frac1{24885},
\]
and the same exact search shows that no four-term decomposition exists.
Round~1 already established
\[
  N(77,79)=6.
\]
Therefore, even at the same denominator \(b=79\), the extremal numerator is \emph{not} \(a=b-1\).
So one cannot reduce Problem~\#304 to the single subfamily \(N(b-1,b)\) without losing information.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Did the proof really force \(x=2\)?}
Yes. The bound \(1/3+1/4+1/5=47/60\) is the maximum possible three-term sum with \(x\ge 3\), and \((p-1)/p\ge 10/11\) for every prime \(p\ge 11\).
\item \textbf{Was the two-term criterion applied in reduced form?}
Yes. For odd prime \(p\), \((p-2,2p)=1\), so the lemma applies exactly as stated.
\item \textbf{Were all divisors of \(4p^2\) considered?}
Yes. Every divisor is \(2^i p^j\) with \(0\le i,j\le 2\), so \(i+j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\).
\item \textbf{Could the computational side remark hide a gap?}
The remark about \(N(78,79)=5\) is finite and exact: the Round~1 algorithm is an exhaustive iterative-deepening search with a safe pruning rule. It found the displayed five-term representation and found no four-term representation.
\item \textbf{Does the theorem solve the original asymptotic problem?}
No. It solves only a prime-denominator boundary subproblem, but it is a genuine new theorem on an infinite family.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{7) FINAL (EXACTLY ONE)}
\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}
Round~2 proves the new theorem that for every prime \(p\ge 11\), the fraction \((p-1)/p\) needs at least four unit fractions.
Equivalently, among primes the relation \(N(p-1,p)\le 3\) occurs exactly for \(p=2,3,5,7\).
In addition, Round~2 proves a concrete obstruction to the simplifying strategy of studying only \((b-1)/b\): already at \(b=79\), the true worst numerator is \(77\), not \(78\).
