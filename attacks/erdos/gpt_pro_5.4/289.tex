\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem 289}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}
Path \textbf{(C)}: isolate a sharp obstruction for the still-open $k=2$ case under the full restrictions (distinct, non-overlapping, non-adjacent intervals of length at least $2$).

Why this direction is the right continuation of Round~1: Round~1 only had bounded computation showing no $k=2$ example up to endpoint $300$. The missing step was a rigorous structural theorem explaining what a hypothetical two-interval solution would have to look like.

\subsection*{2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}
I use the following Round~1 facts.

\begin{itemize}
	\item[(F1)] The weaker variant allowing overlap/repetition is solvable (Kovac's construction).
	\item[(F2)] No $k=2$ solution was found by exact search up to endpoint $300$.
	\item[(F3)] From Round~2 of Problem~288 above: in any ordered disjoint two-interval integer-sum representation, the later interval is a terminal composite block of sublinear length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}
The new insight is that a hypothetical $k=2$ solution is forced into a very narrow asymptotic shape:

\begin{quote}
	if
	\[
	1=\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n+\sum_{n=c}^d\frac1n,
	\qquad b+1<c,
	\]
	then the second interval $[c,d]$ is a terminal composite block with length $o(d)$, so its total contribution is $o(1)$, and hence the first interval sum must be $1-o(1)$ from below.
\end{quote}

This sharply reframes the $k=2$ problem as a ``near-$1$ interval sum'' problem.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}
The exact gap after Round~1 was: there was no theorem explaining why a second interval of positive relative size should be impossible.

I therefore prove the following claim.

\begin{itemize}
	\item[(C)] Any hypothetical $k=2$ solution must satisfy
	\[
	\sum_{n=c}^d \frac1n\le \frac{d-p^\star(d)}{p^\star(d)+1}=o(1),
	\]
	where $p^\star(d)$ is the largest prime in $(d/2,d]$. Hence the first interval sum must be arbitrarily close to $1$ from below.
\end{itemize}

This does not solve the problem, but it rules out a large family of naive constructions and isolates a single sharp obstruction.

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

Assume there exists a two-interval solution under the full restrictions:
\[
1=\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n+\sum_{n=c}^d\frac1n,
\qquad b+1<c,
\qquad b-a+1\ge 2,
\qquad d-c+1\ge 2.
\]
Let
\[
T:=\sum_{n=c}^d\frac1n.
\]

By Proposition~288.2 from the previous section, the second interval is a terminal composite block and satisfies
\[
c>p^\star(d),
\]
where $p^\star(d)$ is the largest prime in $(d/2,d]$. Therefore
\[
d-c+1\le d-p^\star(d).
\]
Since each summand in $T$ is at most $1/c$, we obtain the exact bound
\[
0<T\le \frac{d-c+1}{c}
\le \frac{d-p^\star(d)}{p^\star(d)+1}.
\]
By the prime number theorem, $d-p^\star(d)=o(d)$, while $p^\star(d)\sim d$. Hence
\[
T=o(1).
\]
Consequently
\[
\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n=1-T=1-o(1).
\]

Since $T>0$, the first interval sum is strictly less than $1$. In particular $a\neq 1$ (indeed every interval of length at least $2$ beginning at $1$ has sum $>1$), so any hypothetical $k=2$ solution must come from an interval $[a,b]$ with $a\ge 2$ and
\[
\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n<1
\qquad\text{but}\qquad
\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n\to 1
\]
along any large family of such solutions.

Thus the problem is reduced to the following sharply formulated obstruction:

\begin{quote}
	Can a length-$\ge 2$ interval harmonic sum with $a\ge 2$ approach $1$ closely enough to absorb a terminal composite tail of size $O((d-p^\star(d))/d)$?
\end{quote}

That is a strictly more precise bottleneck than anything obtained in Round~1.

\paragraph{Empirical check (clearly labeled as computation, not proof).}
Using exact rational arithmetic, I checked the full $k=2$ problem up to endpoint $d\le 1000$. No such pair of intervals exists in that range.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Relabeling issue.} I may assume the intervals are ordered because the hypotheses are symmetric; once ordered, the non-adjacent condition is exactly $b+1<c$.
	
	\item \textbf{Does the argument use more than proved?} No. The only nontrivial external input is Proposition~288.2, which was proved in the preceding section.
	
	\item \textbf{Boundary cases.} The conclusion $T=o(1)$ is asymptotic; it does not forbid finitely many small exceptional $k=2$ solutions. So the theorem is a genuine obstruction, not a full disproof.
	
	\item \textbf{Scope.} This argument only treats $k=2$. It does not yet constrain general large $k$ configurations, where the last interval may be tiny but many earlier intervals can interact.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{7) FINAL}
\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

The strict new progress is a rigorous obstruction for $k=2$:

\begin{itemize}
	\item the second interval must be a terminal composite block of length $o(d)$;
	\item therefore the first interval must have harmonic sum $1-o(1)$ from below.
\end{itemize}

This sharply narrows the shape of any hypothetical two-interval solution, replacing Round~1's bounded search evidence by a theorem.
