\section{Problem \#782}

\subsection*{1. ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}

I again invoke the fail-safe ``strict advance'' clause. The main Round-1 gap was that the write-up had only very general upper bounds and obstructions, but not the strongest concrete positive constructions that are actually known. The most promising Round-2 direction is therefore to replace vague local evidence by a rigorous infinite construction: infinitely many length-$4$ quasi-progressions of squares with width $1$.

\subsection*{2. Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}

I use the following Round-1 material without re-proving it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The formal restatement of the two questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(Q1)] Is there an absolute $C$ such that the squares contain quasi-progressions of width $C$ and arbitrarily large length?
\item[(Q2)] Do the squares contain Hilbert cubes of arbitrarily large dimension?
\end{enumerate}
\item The unconditional Hilbert-cube upper bound from Dietmann--Elsholtz:
if
\[
H(a;b_1,\dots,b_d)\subseteq \{m^2:m\geq 1\}\cap[1,N],
\]
then
\[
d \leq 7 \log\log N
\]
for large $N$.
\item The conditional obstruction from Cilleruelo--Granville: Bombieri--Lang-type conjectures would imply a negative answer to arbitrarily large affine cubes in the squares.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{3. NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND 2)}

The genuinely new ingredient in this round is the 2004 paper of Brown--Freedman--Shiue [BFS04], which gives much sharper positive information than Round 1 recorded:

\begin{enumerate}
\item There are \emph{infinitely many} $4$-term quasi-progressions of squares with width $1$ (their Theorem~3.1).
\item They also exhibit explicit $5$-term examples of width $6$.
\item They prove that the squares contain arbitrarily long semi-progressions of width asymptotic to $\frac{3}{2}n$ (their Theorem~4.1), which is far weaker than fixed-width quasi-progressions but is still a genuine long-configuration theorem.
\item They conjecture that for each fixed $K$ there is an $N(K)$ such that no $n$-term $K$-quasi-progression of squares exists once $n \geq N(K)$.
\end{enumerate}

Thus the Round-2 improvement is not just ``more context'': it installs a rigorous infinite positive family at width $1$, while also sharpening the obstruction picture for fixed width.

\subsection*{4. ATTACK PLAN (ROUND 2)}

Round 1 left two serious gaps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item It did not isolate the strongest known positive theorem for quasi-progressions in the squares.
\item It did not rigorously explain any nontrivial family of such progressions.
\end{enumerate}

The Round-2 plan is to prove the infinite $4$-term width-$1$ family carefully from the Pell-equation construction in [BFS04], and then explain why this still falls well short of settling (Q1) or (Q2).

\subsection*{5. WORK (ROUND 2)}

\paragraph{5.1 A rigorous infinite family of $4$-QP$(1)$'s in the squares.}

I write $4$-QP$(1)$ for a quasi-progression of length $4$ and width $1$.

\medskip

Take any Pythagorean triple
\[
a^2+b^2=c^2
\qquad (0<a<b).
\]
Then
\[
c^2-(b-a)^2=(a^2+b^2)-(b^2-2ab+a^2)=2ab,
\]
and
\[
(b+a)^2-c^2=(b^2+2ab+a^2)-(a^2+b^2)=2ab.
\]
Therefore
\[
(b-a)^2,\ c^2,\ (b+a)^2
\]
is a $3$-term arithmetic progression of squares with common difference $2ab$.

Now define
\[
D:=(b+a)^2+2ab=a^2+4ab+b^2.
\]
If $D$ were a square, then
\[
(b-a)^2,\ c^2,\ (b+a)^2,\ D
\]
would be a $4$-term arithmetic progression of squares, which is impossible. Hence $D$ is a positive nonsquare integer.

Therefore the Pell equation
\[
x^2-Dy^2=1
\]
has infinitely many positive integer solutions $(x,y)$.

For any such solution with $y\geq 1$, consider the four squares
\[
X_1=(b-a)^2y^2,\qquad
X_2=c^2y^2,\qquad
X_3=(b+a)^2y^2,\qquad
X_4=x^2.
\]
These are strictly increasing, because
\[
(b-a)^2<c^2<(b+a)^2<D
\]
and
\[
x^2=Dy^2+1>(b+a)^2y^2.
\]

Now compute the consecutive differences:
\[
X_2-X_1=\bigl(c^2-(b-a)^2\bigr)y^2=2ab\,y^2,
\]
\[
X_3-X_2=\bigl((b+a)^2-c^2\bigr)y^2=2ab\,y^2,
\]
and
\[
X_4-X_3=x^2-(b+a)^2y^2=(Dy^2+1)-(b+a)^2y^2=2ab\,y^2+1.
\]
So the three consecutive differences are
\[
2ab\,y^2,\quad 2ab\,y^2,\quad 2ab\,y^2+1.
\]
They all lie in an interval of the form $[d,d+1]$, namely with
\[
d=2ab\,y^2.
\]
Hence
\[
(X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4)
\]
is a $4$-term quasi-progression of width $1$ consisting entirely of squares.

Since the Pell equation has infinitely many positive solutions, this yields infinitely many $4$-QP$(1)$'s in the squares.

\medskip

So the following theorem is now rigorously established inside this Round-2 write-up:

\[
\mbox{There exist infinitely many }4\mbox{-term quasi-progressions of squares of width }1.
\]

\paragraph{5.2 A concrete example.}

Taking the primitive Pythagorean triple $(a,b,c)=(3,4,5)$ gives
\[
(b-a)^2=1,\qquad c^2=25,\qquad (b+a)^2=49,\qquad D=73.
\]
A Pell solution of
\[
x^2-73y^2=1
\]
is
\[
(x,y)=(1068,125).
\]
Therefore
\[
125^2,\ 625^2,\ 875^2,\ 1068^2
\]
is a $4$-QP$(1)$ of squares. Indeed, the consecutive gaps are
\[
24\cdot 125^2,\quad 24\cdot 125^2,\quad 24\cdot 125^2+1.
\]

\paragraph{5.3 What this does and does not settle.}

This infinite family is a real advance over Round 1, but it does \emph{not} settle (Q1). It only gives length $4$, not arbitrarily large length.

Brown--Freedman--Shiue also report explicit $5$-term examples of width $6$, for instance
\[
1^2,\ 41^2,\ 58^2,\ 71^2,\ 82^2,
\]
and
\[
10^2,\ 25^2,\ 34^2,\ 41^2,\ 47^2.
\]
So the best concrete positive picture from the checked sources is:

\begin{itemize}
\item width $1$: arbitrarily many examples of length $4$;
\item width $6$: explicit examples of length $5$;
\item semi-progressions: arbitrary length with much larger width.
\end{itemize}

But there is still no fixed $C$ known to support arbitrarily large length, and the Hilbert-cube question remains open as well.

\subsection*{6. ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

I checked the main places where the new argument could fail.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Could $D$ accidentally be a square?}  
If it were, we would obtain a $4$-term arithmetic progression of squares, which is impossible. So $D$ is indeed a nonsquare and the Pell equation is nondegenerate.

\item \emph{Are the four numbers really increasing?}  
Yes. The first three are already increasing because they come from a nontrivial Pythagorean triple. The last inequality follows from
\[
x^2=Dy^2+1>(b+a)^2y^2.
\]

\item \emph{Do the differences really have width $1$ rather than just being close?}  
Yes. The first two gaps are exactly equal to $2ab\,y^2$, while the third is exactly one larger.

\item \emph{Does this imply anything about arbitrarily long fixed-width quasi-progressions?}  
No. The construction gives infinitely many examples of one fixed length, namely $4$. It does not address the unbounded-length part of (Q1).

\item \emph{Does it imply arbitrarily large Hilbert cubes in the squares?}  
No. Again, the construction is one-dimensional and length-bounded.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{7. FINAL}

\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

The substantive Round-2 advance is the installation of a rigorous infinite family:
\[
\mbox{infinitely many }4\mbox{-term width-}1\mbox{ quasi-progressions of squares}.
\]

This sharply improves the positive side of the picture compared with Round 1. Nevertheless:

\begin{enumerate}
\item (Q1) remains open: no fixed width $C$ is known to support arbitrarily long quasi-progressions in the squares.
\item (Q2) remains open: no arbitrarily large Hilbert cubes in the squares are known, while the best unconditional upper bound in the checked sources is still
\[
d \leq 7\log\log N.
\]
\end{enumerate}
