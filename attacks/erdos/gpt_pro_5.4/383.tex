
\section{Problem \#383 (Round 2)}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}

Path A (proof-oriented constructive progress). Round 1 had examples through $k=6$ and no example for $k=7$ in the search range used there. The new aim is to push the exact search further and replace ``none found'' by explicit rigorous constructions for larger $k$.

\subsection*{2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}

I use the Round-1 reformulation:

for a prime $p>k$,
\[
P\!\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(p^2+i)\right)=p
\]
is equivalent to
\[
P(p^2+i)\le p\qquad (1\le i\le k).
\]

So it is enough to exhibit primes $p$ such that each of the integers
\[
p^2+1,\dots,p^2+k
\]
is $p$-smooth.

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}

The new ingredient is not a new theorem but a new exact computational certificate: I found and verified explicit primes for
\[
k=7,\ 8,\ 9.
\]
This strictly advances Round 1, which only had examples through $k=6$.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}

The exact Round-1 gap was the absence of any example for $k=7$ in the earlier search range. The new plan is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item search much farther than Round 1;
\item for each successful prime $p$, give exact factorisations of $p^2+i$ for all $1\le i\le k$;
\item conclude rigorously that the largest prime factor of the whole product is exactly $p$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

\paragraph{Example for $k=7$.}
Take
\[
p=510569.
\]
Then the exact factorisations are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $p^2+1=2\cdot 37\cdot 31721\cdot 111053$;
\item $p^2+2=3\cdot 2731\cdot 4651\cdot 6841$;
\item $p^2+3=2^2\cdot 13\cdot 43\cdot 523\cdot 222913$;
\item $p^2+4=5\cdot 17\cdot 29^2\cdot 641\cdot 5689$;
\item $p^2+5=2\cdot 3\cdot 7\cdot 23\cdot 167\cdot 1061\cdot 1523$;
\item $p^2+6=11\cdot 59\cdot 101\cdot 991\cdot 4013$;
\item $p^2+7=2^3\cdot 71\cdot 401\cdot 449\cdot 2549$.
\end{enumerate}

Every prime shown is $<p$. Since $p>7$, none of $p^2+1,\dots,p^2+7$ is divisible by $p$. Therefore the factor $p^2$ contributes the only copies of $p$ to
\[
\prod_{i=0}^{7}(p^2+i),
\]
and all other prime divisors are $<p$. Hence
\[
P\!\left(\prod_{i=0}^{7}(p^2+i)\right)=p.
\]

\item $p^2+2=3^6\cdot 82129\cdot 137491$;
\item $p^2+3=2^2\cdot 7\cdot 31\cdot 5449\cdot 1740451$;
\item $p^2+4=13\cdot 251969\cdot 2513089$;

\end{enumerate}

Again every displayed prime is $<p$, and $p>8$ implies $p$ does not divide $p^2+i$ for $1\le i\le 8$. Therefore
\[
P\!\left(\prod_{i=0}^{8}(p^2+i)\right)=p.
\]

All displayed prime divisors are $<p$, and $p>9$ implies $p$ does not divide $p^2+i$ for $1\le i\le 9$. Hence
\[
P\!\left(\prod_{i=0}^{9}(p^2+i)\right)=p.
\]

\paragraph{Consequences for Problem \#382.}
Each of the three examples above also gives a bad interval:
\[
[p^2,p^2+7],\qquad [p^2,p^2+8],\qquad [p^2,p^2+9].
\]
So the Problem \#383 search now yields rigorous constructions for bad intervals of lengths $7,8,9$.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

I checked the key logical points.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Could one of the numbers $p^2+i$ be divisible by $p$?} No, because $1\le i\le k<p$ in all three examples, so $p$ does not divide $p^2+i$.
\item \emph{Is it enough to factor only $p^2+1,\dots,p^2+k$?} Yes. The factor $p^2$ already contributes the prime $p$, and once all other factors are shown to have prime divisors $<p$, the largest prime divisor of the full product is exactly $p$.
\item \emph{Could there be a larger hidden prime factor in one of the listed numbers?} No. The displayed decompositions are exact factorisations.
\end{enumerate}
