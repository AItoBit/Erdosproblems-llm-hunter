\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem \#316: two bins of unit fractions}
\noindent Complete reconstruction of S\'andor's counterexample, 23 September 2026.

\subsection*{1) Statement}
If a finite set $A\subset\mathbb N\setminus\{1\}$ satisfies $\sum_{d\in A}1/d<2$, must it be partitionable into two sets, each with reciprocal sum strictly less than one? The answer is no. The strict inequalities matter, and the counterexample uses distinct denominators.

\subsection*{2) The explicit denominator set and its sum}
Take all divisors of 120 strictly between 1 and 120:
\[
 A=\{2,3,4,5,6,8,10,12,15,20,24,30,40,60\}.
\]
In particular neither denominator one nor denominator 120 is included. Multiplication by 120 gives the integer weights
\[
 \{60,40,30,24,20,15,12,10,8,6,5,4,3,2\},
\]
whose sum is $239$. Equivalently, the divisor sum is
\[
 \sigma(120)=(1+2+4+8)(1+3)(1+5)=360,
\]
so the sum of reciprocal divisors after removing the terms for 1 and 120 is $360/120-1-1/120=239/120<2$.

\subsection*{3) The denominator lattice prevents a partition}
Every subset sum of the reciprocals in $A$ is an integer multiple of $1/120$. If such a sum is strictly less than one, it is at most $119/120$. Therefore two parts each satisfying the required strict bound would have total at most
\[
 \frac{119}{120}+\frac{119}{120}=\frac{238}{120},
\]
contradicting the actual total $239/120$. This argument includes empty parts and exhausts all possible partitions without a subset search.

More generally, if every denominator of a finite set divides an integer $L$, any partition into $r$ parts of reciprocal sum less than one forces total sum at most $r-r/L$. Thus any total strictly between $r-r/L$ and $r$ gives an obstruction. This is a criterion; no construction for every $r$ is inferred from the single example above.

\subsection*{4) Outcome and source}
\textbf{SOLVED by an explicit counterexample.} The total, distinctness, exclusion of denominator one and impossibility of every two-part partition are proved exactly. This is S\'andor's known example, not a new counterexample or a claim that fourteen denominators are minimal.
