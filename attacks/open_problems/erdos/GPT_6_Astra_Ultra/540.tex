\textbf{Erd\H{o}s problem 540.}
Does $|A|\geq C\sqrt N$, for a suitable absolute constant $C$, force a nonempty zero-sum subset of $A\subseteq\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$?

\textbf{Status and precise known input.}
Yes. We use Hamidoune and Z\'emor, \emph{On zero-free subset sums}, Acta Arithmetica 78 (1996), 143--152, Theorem 4.5:
\texttt{https://matwbn.icm.edu.pl/ksiazki/aa/aa78/aa7823.pdf}.
Their theorem, in its asymptotic consequence, says that every subset of any finite abelian group of order $N$ of size at least
\[
 \sqrt{2N}+O(N^{1/3}\log N)
\]
has a nonempty zero-sum subset, with an absolute implied constant in the sufficient threshold. This is an external theorem, not a bound derived from the pigeonhole principle alone.
The problem source is \texttt{https://www.erdosproblems.com/540}; checked 24 September 2026.

\textbf{The quantifiers and the cyclic lower bound.}
Since $N^{1/3}\log N=o(\sqrt N)$, for every fixed $\eta>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$, size at least $(\sqrt2+\eta)\sqrt N$ suffices. In particular an absolute $C$ works for all large $N$. Enlarging it covers the finitely many smaller $N$: a set equal to the whole group contains $0$, and for thresholds exceeding $N$ the implication is vacuous.

This leading constant cannot be decreased in general. Let $k$ be the largest integer satisfying $k(k+1)/2<N$, and take the distinct residues
\[
 A=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}\subseteq\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z.
\]
Every nonempty subset has an ordinary integer sum between $1$ and $k(k+1)/2<N$, hence is not zero modulo $N$. Furthermore $k=\sqrt{2N}+O(1)$. Thus a universal threshold $(c+o(1))\sqrt N$ with $c<\sqrt2$ would fail for all sufficiently large $N$. Together with the theorem, this identifies the leading constant for cyclic groups.

\textbf{Why distinctness is crucial.}
For a sequence, $N-1$ copies of the residue $1$ have no nonempty zero sum. Thus the square-root statement could not hold for sequences permitting repetitions.
Conversely, every sequence of $N$ group elements has a nonempty zero-sum consecutive block: its $N+1$ partial sums, including the empty sum, repeat in a group of order $N$. This proves only a linear threshold and explains why it does not supply the theorem used above.

\textbf{An elementary necessary condition for a counterexample.}
If $0\in A$, there is a singleton witness. If $a,-a\in A$ are distinct, there is a two-element witness. Thus a zero-sum-free set contains at most one element from each two-element orbit of negation. Elements of order two are fixed points of negation and may occur individually: the relation $a+a=0$ does not use a subset of a set unless there are two distinct available elements, so it does not exclude these fixed points.
If $h$ is the number of nonzero elements of order two, this elementary argument gives only $|A|\leq(N-1-h)/2+h$, far larger than the square-root theorem in general. It identifies precisely the limitation of this shortcut.

\textbf{Completion estimate.}
The desired universal bound and its sharp leading constant follow from the stated external theorem and the fully proved lower construction.
