\section*{Erd\H{o}s Problem 793: the second term for strongly primitive sets}

\paragraph{Statement and claim.}
Let \(F(n)\) be the largest size of \(A\subseteq[1,n]\cap\mathbb N\)
such that \(a\nmid bc\) whenever \(a,b,c\in A\),
\(a\ne b\), and \(a\ne c\). The factors \(b,c\) may coincide.
Then
\[
 F(n)=\pi(n)+\left(\frac{27}{2}+o(1)\right)
                    \frac{n^{2/3}}{(\log n)^2}.
                                                               \tag{1}
\]
We reconstruct the AI-assisted argument published by
Przemek Chojecki, \emph{The second term for strongly
2-primitive sets},
\url{https://www.ulam.ai/research/erdos793.pdf}.
The attribution and current statement are at
\url{https://www.erdosproblems.com/793}.
The substantial external analytic input is the prime
number theorem and its usual partial-summation consequences.
The earlier GPT-5.2 file uses a different three-distinct-elements
formulation; we retain the present statement, including \(b=c\).

\paragraph{A private-factor lemma.}
Suppose every \(a\in A\) has a chosen factorization \(a=uv\)
with \(u,v\) in a common set \(B\). Regard \(\{u,v\}\)
as a multiset, and let \(\mu_a(w)\) be its multiplicity.
Some factor \(w\) of this multiset has
\[
 \mu_a(w)>\mu_{a'}(w)\quad\hbox{for every }a'\in A\setminus\{a\}.
                                                               \tag{2}
\]
If \(u\ne v\) and this fails, choose another chosen
factorization containing \(u\) and another containing \(v\).
Their products \(b,c\ne a\) satisfy \(a\mid bc\), even if
they coincide, a contradiction.
If \(u=v\), multiplicity two is unique: another such
factorization would have the same product \(a=u^2\).
Selecting one factor satisfying (2) for each \(a\)
gives an injection into \(B\), since two strict opposite
multiplicity inequalities cannot both hold. Thus
\[
 |A|\leq|B|.                                                   \tag{3}
\]

\paragraph{An economical multiplicative basis.}
Put \(y=n^{1/3}\), \(M=y/\log n\), and \(S=M^2\).
Use the union of these four sets:
\[
 \begin{split}
 B_0&=[1,n^{3/5}]\cap\mathbb N,\\
 B_1&=\{p\text{ prime}:n^{3/5}<p\leq n\},\\
 B_2&=\{pq:p,q\text{ prime},\ p,q\leq y\},\\
 B_3&=\{qr:q,r\text{ prime},\ y<q\leq n^{2/5},\
                                      r\leq n/q^2\}.
 \end{split}                                                   \tag{4}
\]
Every integer \(m\leq n\) is a product of two members of this union.
First note that every \(m\leq X\) factors into a number at
most \(X^{2/3}\) and a factor which is either prime or at
most \(X^{2/3}\).
If a prime factor exceeds \(X^{1/3}\), take it as one factor.
Otherwise multiply small prime factors until their product
first exceeds \(X^{1/3}\); both it and the complementary
factor are at most \(X^{2/3}\).
The case \(m\leq X^{2/3}\) is immediate using \(1\).

For \(m\leq n^{9/10}\), apply this observation with
\(X=n^{9/10}\), obtaining factors in \(B_0\cup B_1\).
For larger \(m\), a prime factor \(p>n^{2/5}\) gives
\(m/p<n^{3/5}\), again finishing the factorization.
We may therefore assume all prime factors are at most \(n^{2/5}\).

Count, with multiplicity, those exceeding \(n^{1/5}\).
If there are at most two, a divisor
\(u\in[n^{2/5},n^{3/5}]\) is obtained by starting with
the largest of them, or \(1\), and multiplying small factors.
If there are two large factors, omit the other one:
the available product still exceeds \(n^{1/2}\), so the
crossing occurs. The last multiplier is at most \(n^{1/5}\).
Thus both \(u,m/u\) lie in \(B_0\).
If there are at least three, let \(p\geq q\geq r>n^{1/5}\)
be the three largest, with multiplicity.
For \(q\leq y\), use \(qr\in B_2\) and \(m/(qr)<n^{3/5}\).
For \(q>y\), the inequalities
\(r\leq n/(pq)\leq n/q^2\) give \(qr\in B_3\), and
\(m/(qr)<n^{7/15}<n^{3/5}\).
This proves the basis assertion.

\paragraph{Counting the upper-bound basis.}
Clearly
\[
 |B_0|+|B_1|=\pi(n)+o(S),\qquad
 |B_2|=\binom{\pi(y)+1}{2}=(9/2+o(1))S.
\]
In \(B_3\), the factor \(q>y\) is unique because \(r<y\).
For any fixed \(T>1\), the prime number theorem gives
\[
 \sum_{y<q\leq Ty}\pi(n/q^2)
 =\left(9(1-1/T)+o(1)\right)S.                                \tag{5}
\]
Indeed, uniformly in that interval,
\(\pi(n/q^2)=(3+o(1))n/(q^2\log n)\), while partial summation
gives
\(\sum_{y<q\leq Ty}q^{-2}
 =(3+o(1))(1-1/T)/(y\log n)\).
For the remaining range \(Ty<q\leq n^{2/5}\), the standard
bounds \(\pi(z)\ll z/\log z\) and
\(\sum_{q>z}q^{-2}\ll1/(z\log z)\) give \(O(S/T)\).
Letting first \(n\to\infty\), then \(T\to\infty\),
we obtain \(|B_3|=(9+o(1))S\).
Equations (3)--(5) prove the upper half of (1).

\paragraph{Linear triples supply the lower bound.}
Suppose \(\mathcal H\) is a family of triples of distinct
primes whose products are at most \(n\), with any two
triples sharing at most one prime.
Retain all primes at most \(n\) outside \(V(\mathcal H)\),
and replace the used primes by the triple products.
The resulting set is strongly \(2\)-primitive.
A retained prime divides no other element.
For a target triple product, each other triple product
contains at most one of its three distinct primes;
even two such products, including a repeated one, cannot
supply all three. Retained primes supply none.
The resulting cardinality is
\[
 \pi(n)-|V(\mathcal H)|+|\mathcal H|.                           \tag{6}
\]

\paragraph{Packing the prime triples in logarithmic bins.}
Fix \(h>0\), put \(t=e^h\), and set
\(\Delta_i=t^{i+1}-t^i\) for \(i\in\mathbb Z\).
Consider cells \((i,j)\) with
\[
 i\leq j,\quad i+2j\leq-4,\quad k=-i-j-3.
\]
Then \(k>j\).
Choose for now a fixed finite collection of these cells.
For every occurring index \(r\), let \(P_r\) be the primes
in \((yt^r,yt^{r+1}]\), of size
\[
 m_r=(3+o(1))M\Delta_r.
\]
Since \(\Delta_k\geq t\Delta_j\geq t\Delta_i\), for large
\(n\) we have \(m_k\geq\max(m_i,m_j)\).

For \(i<j\), properly edge-colour the complete bipartite
graph between \(P_i,P_j\) with \(\max(m_i,m_j)\) colours,
and label its colours by different primes in \(P_k\).
For \(i=j\), properly edge-colour the complete graph on
\(P_i\) with at most \(m_i\) colours and label them by primes
in \(P_k\).
These colourings can be constructed explicitly.
For a bipartite graph, label both sides injectively in
\(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\), and use the difference of labels.
For a complete graph of odd order \(m\), use colour
\((a+b)/2\) modulo \(m\).
For even \(m\), use vertices
\(\{\infty\}\cup\mathbb Z/(m-1)\mathbb Z\), colour
\(\{\infty,a\}\) by \(a\), and the other edges by
\((a+b)/2\) modulo \(m-1\).

An edge \(\{p,q\}\) with colour prime \(r\) becomes the
triple \(\{p,q,r\}\).
Its product is at most
\(y^3t^{i+j+k+3}=n\).
Within one cell no prime pair repeats, because each colour
class is a matching.
Across different cells, two shared bin indices determine
the third index from their sum \(-3\); sorting determines
the cell uniquely. If the shared primes occupy one bin,
both cells must be the same diagonal cell.
Thus all the triples together form a linear family.
The number of triples, divided by \(M^2\), tends to
\[
 9\sum_{\substack{(i,j)\text{ chosen}\\i<j}}\Delta_i\Delta_j
 +\frac92\sum_{(i,i)\text{ chosen}}\Delta_i^2.                  \tag{7}
\]
Only finitely many bins are used, so the number of used
primes is \(O(M)=o(S)\).

\paragraph{The constant \(27/2\).}
Over all allowed cells, the off-diagonal sum is
\[
 \sum_{j\leq-1}\Delta_j t^j+
 \sum_{j\geq0}\Delta_j t^{-2j-3}
 =\frac1{t+1}+t^{-2}.
\]
The diagonal half-sum is
\[
 \frac12\sum_{i\leq-2}\Delta_i^2
 =\frac{t-1}{2t^2(t+1)}.
\]
Their sum is \(t^{-1}+\tfrac12t^{-2}\), tending to \(3/2\)
as \(h\downarrow0\).
Given \(\varepsilon>0\), first choose \(h\) small, and
then a finite collection of cells whose positive weights
make (7) greater than \(27/2-\varepsilon\).
Only then let \(n\) grow.
Equation (6) supplies sets of size
\[
 \pi(n)+(27/2-\varepsilon-o(1))S.
\]
Since \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, this matches the upper
bound and proves (1). Keeping this order of the three
choices avoids requiring a prime number theorem uniform
over a growing collection of bins.
