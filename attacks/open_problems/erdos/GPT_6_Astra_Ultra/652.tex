\section*{Erd\H{o}s Problem 652: the \(k\)-th smallest pinned distance count}

\paragraph{Statement and claim.}
For \(n\) distinct planar points, let \(R(x)\) count the distinct
positive distances from \(x\) to the other points, and order the
points so that \(R(x_1)\leq\cdots\leq R(x_n)\).
The problem asks whether the best constants \(\alpha_k\) permitting
\(R(x_k)<\alpha_k\sqrt n\) for arbitrarily large configurations
(or for every sufficiently large \(n\)) must tend to infinity.
In fact, for \(k\geq2\),
\[
 \alpha_k=\Theta(\sqrt k).
\]
We reconstruct the pinned crossing-number argument and circle-grid
construction in Surya Mathialagan,
\emph{On Bipartite Distinct Distances in the Plane},
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01883}, Sections 2--3.
In the arXiv version the pinned assertion is Theorem 3.6;
its Theorem 1.4 only states the weaker unpinned consequence.
See \url{https://www.erdosproblems.com/652}.

\paragraph{External geometric inputs.}
We use two standard theorems, in the forms stated in the cited paper.
A topological multigraph with \(v\) vertices, \(e>5\mu v\) edges,
and maximum edge multiplicity \(\mu\) has crossing number at least
\(c e^3/(\mu v^2)\).
Also, among \(m\) planar points the number of lines containing at
least \(r\geq2\) of them is at most
\[
 C\left(\frac{m^2}{r^3}+\frac mr\right).
\]
The latter is the rich-line consequence of the
Szemer\'edi--Trotter theorem. Absolute constants below may depend
on these two absolute theorem constants, not on \(m,N\).

\paragraph{The pinned estimate.}
Let \(P,Q\) be disjoint planar point sets of sizes \(m,N\), with
\(2\leq m\leq N^{1/3}\), and put
\[
 t=\max_{p\in P}\#\{|p-q|:q\in Q\}.
\]
We prove \(t\geq c\sqrt{mN}\).
For bounded \(m\), this follows already from two centres:
each ordered pair of distances from two distinct centres specifies
the intersection of two circles, containing at most two points.
Hence \(N\leq2t^2\).
We may therefore assume \(m\) is larger than a sufficiently large
absolute constant.

Suppose for contradiction that \(t<\varepsilon\sqrt{mN}\),
where the positive absolute \(\varepsilon\) will be small.
Draw all circles about each \(p\in P\) passing through \(Q\);
there are at most \(mt\) circles.
On every circle containing at least three points of \(Q\),
join consecutive points by the intervening circular arcs.
This gives a multigraph on \(Q\) with
\[
 e\geq mN-2mt
\]
edges, since circles with one or two points discard at most \(2mt\)
point--circle incidences.
Distinct circles meet at most twice, and arcs from the same circle
have disjoint interiors. After small local perturbations if needed,
this drawing has \(O(m^2t^2)\) crossings.

We bound the number of edges of high multiplicity.
The centres of circles supplying arcs between a fixed pair \(u,v\)
all lie on the perpendicular bisector \(\ell\) of that pair.
Because circles with at most two points were discarded, any one
circle supplies at most one arc with these endpoints.
Thus multiplicity at least \(r\) forces \(\ell\) to contain at least
\(r\) points of \(P\).

A line containing \(s\) centres accounts for at most \(2st\) such
arcs: each of the at most \(st\) circles has at most two consecutive
arcs whose endpoints are exchanged by reflection in the line.
Each such arc crosses one of the two intersection points of the
circle with that line, which proves this count.
Divide the possible \(s\)'s into dyadic ranges and apply the rich-line
bound. The number of arcs whose bisector contains at least \(r\)
centres is therefore
\[
 O\left(\frac{tm^2}{r^2}+tm\log(2m)\right).                      \tag{1}
\]
Indeed a dyadic range \(s\leq|\ell\cap P|<2s\) contributes
\(O(t(m^2/s^2+m))\); summing proves (1).

Fix a sufficiently large absolute integer \(K\), and remove every
edge belonging to a pair of multiplicity at least \(K\).
By (1), the number removed is at most
\[
 C'\left(\frac{tm^2}{K^2}+tm\log(2m)\right)
 \leq C'\varepsilon mN
       \left(\frac1{K^2}+\frac{\log(2m)}m\right),
\]
where \(m^3\leq N\) was used.
Choosing \(\varepsilon\) sufficiently small leaves
\(e'\geq mN/2\) edges, of multiplicity at most \(K\).
For \(m>10K\), the crossing lemma applies and gives
\[
 m^2t^2\gg \operatorname{cr}(G')
       \gg\frac{(mN)^3}{K N^2}
       \gg m^3N.
\]
Thus \(t\gg\sqrt{mN}\), contradicting the choice of
\(\varepsilon\) smaller than this absolute constant.
The bounded range \(m\leq10K\) was covered by the two-circle argument.
This proves the uniform pinned estimate.

\paragraph{The lower bound for \(\alpha_k\).}
Take \(P=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}\), the \(k\) points with smallest
distance counts, and let \(Q\) consist of the other \(n-k\) points.
For fixed \(k\) and \(n\geq k^3+k\), the estimate applies and gives
some \(p\in P\) with at least \(c\sqrt{k(n-k)}\) distances to \(Q\).
Every point of \(P\) has \(R(p)\leq R(x_k)\), so
\[
 R(x_k)\geq c\sqrt{k(n-k)}.
\]
As \(n\to\infty\), any admissible constant \(\alpha_k\) is therefore
at least \(c\sqrt k\). This proves the requested divergence.

\paragraph{A matching order of magnitude.}
For integers \(s\geq k\), use centres
\[
 P=\{(a,0):1\leq a\leq k\}
\]
and the \(ks^2\) points
\[
 Q=\{(i,\sqrt j):1\leq i\leq s,\
          s^2+1-i^2\leq j\leq s^2+ks-i^2,\ j\in\mathbb Z\}.
\]
All radicands are positive, and these points are distinct.
The squared distance from \((a,0)\) to \((i,\sqrt j)\) is
\(a^2-2ai+i^2+j\).
For all \(1\leq a\leq k\leq s\), these integers lie between
\[
 k^2-2ks+s^2+1
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 k^2-2k+s^2+ks.
\]
There are at most \(3ks\) possible values. Adding the other centres
contributes at most \(k-1\) further distances to each centre.

For an arbitrary sufficiently large \(n\), choose
\(s=\lceil\sqrt{(n-k)/k}\rceil\), and delete points of \(Q\)
until exactly \(n-k\) remain. The \(k\) centres still have
at most \(3ks+k=O(\sqrt{kn})\) distances each.
Thus \(R(x_k)=O(\sqrt{kn})\), with an absolute implied constant,
for every sufficiently large \(n\).
Together with the lower bound this proves
\(\alpha_k=\Theta(\sqrt k)\).

This is a reconstruction of the known resolution using the two
explicit geometric theorems, including the required uniform
constant and the order-statistic quantifiers.
