\section*{Erd\H{o}s Problem 645: a three-term progression whose step exceeds its first term}

\paragraph{Statement.}
Must every two-colouring of the positive integers contain a monochromatic
progression \(x,x+d,x+2d\) with \(d>x\)?

\paragraph{Claim and attribution.}
Yes. The elementary propagation argument below reconstructs the proof attributed
to Ryan Alweiss on \url{https://www.erdosproblems.com/645}.
It also proves the concrete finite statement that \(\{1,\ldots,200\}\) suffices.
This is a reconstruction of a known solution.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Suppose that there is no such progression among the first \(200\) integers.
Exchange the two colours so that \(1\) is red. Since \(1,3,5\) has
step \(2>1\), at least one of \(3,5\) is blue.
Whenever \(n\geq3\) is red and \(2n-1\leq200\), the progression
\[
 1,\ n,\ 2n-1
\]
forces \(2n-1\) to be blue.

First suppose that \(3\) is blue. If \(n\geq6\) is red and
\(2n-1\leq200\), then \(3,n+1,2n-1\) has step \(n-2>3\).
Its two endpoints are blue, so \(n+1\) is red. If every integer in
\([6,20]\) were blue, the progression \(6,13,20\) would already give a
contradiction. Otherwise choose a red \(x\in[6,20]\). The propagation just
proved makes every integer from \(x\) through \(3x+2\) red: throughout the
induction the largest auxiliary integer required is
\(2(3x+1)-1\leq121\). Thus
\[
 x,\ 2x+1,\ 3x+2
\]
is red and has step \(x+1>x\), a contradiction.

It remains to consider the case where \(5\) is blue. For a red \(n\geq9\)
with \(2n-1\leq200\), the progression
\[
 5,\ n+2,\ 2n-1
\]
has step \(n-3>5\), and forces \(n+2\) to be red.
If every integer in \([9,31]\) were blue, use \(9,20,31\).
Otherwise choose a red \(x\in[9,31]\), and let \(d\) be the smallest
even integer greater than \(x\). Then \(x<d\leq x+2\), and repeated
propagation makes \(x,x+d,x+2d\) red. Indeed, these integers have the
same parity, \(x+2d\leq3x+4\leq97\), and each propagation step uses an
auxiliary integer at most \(2\cdot95-1=189\). This is the final
contradiction. The threshold \(n\geq9\) ensures the required strict
inequality \(n-3>5\).

\paragraph{Scope.}
The requested two-colour assertion is fully proved, with an explicit
finite bound. No assertion about more colours or longer progressions
is needed.
