\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem 67: discrepancy on homogeneous progressions}

\subsection{1) Statement and scope}
For every $f:\mathbb N\to\{-1,1\}$, is
\[
 \sup_{d,m\geq1}\left|\sum_{j=1}^m f(jd)\right|=\infty?
\]
The answer is affirmative by Tao's discrepancy theorem. This note uses
its stronger Hilbert-space formulation as an external theorem, gives the
precise scalar and finite deductions, and proves the first nontrivial
finite obstruction directly. It does not claim a new proof of Tao's
analytic theorem.

\subsection{2) External input and deduction}
Tao proves that if $v_n$ are unit vectors in a real or complex Hilbert
space, then the norms of $\sum_{j=1}^m v_{jd}$, over positive integers
$d,m$, are unbounded. Apply this with the Hilbert space $\mathbb R$
and $v_n=f(n)$. Norm is absolute value, so this proves exactly the
assertion, with no multiplicativity or periodicity hypothesis.

It also implies the following uniform finite statement. For each real
$C>0$ there is an integer $N(C)$ such that every sign sequence on
$[N(C)]$ has a homogeneous progression whose sum has absolute value
greater than $C$. Suppose otherwise. Form the rooted binary tree of
finite sign sequences for which all such sums have absolute value at
most $C$. Every prefix of a vertex is again a vertex, and arbitrarily
deep vertices exist. Starting at the root, choose a child with arbitrarily
deep descendants; such a child exists because there are only two children.
Iterating constructs an infinite sequence all of whose finite prefixes
satisfy the bound. Each homogeneous progression lies in one prefix,
contradicting the scalar theorem. This argument proves existence of
$N(C)$; it does not supply an efficient bound for it.

\subsection{3) An elementary certificate at discrepancy one}
Assume all homogeneous progression sums inside $[12]$ have absolute
value at most $1$. Every even-length such sum is an even integer in
$[-1,1]$, hence zero. Subtracting successive even-length sums gives
\[
 f((2r-1)d)+f(2rd)=0\quad\text{whenever }2rd\leq12.
\]
Changing every sign if necessary, let $f(1)=1$. The displayed relations
give, successively,
\[
 f(2)=-1,\quad f(4)=1,\quad f(8)=-1,\quad
 f(6)=1,\quad f(3)=f(5)=-1.
\]
The relation on $\{5,10\}$ gives $f(10)=1$, so that on $\{9,10\}$
gives $f(9)=-1$. Now $\{9,12\}$ forces $f(12)=1$, whereas
$\{6,12\}$ forces $f(12)=-1$, a contradiction. Thus $N(1)\leq12$.
The paired positions used here are respectively consecutive pairs
inside a homogeneous progression, not arbitrary equal-spaced pairs.

There is a further elementary special case. If $f$ is eventually
periodic with period $q$, then $f(kq)$ is a fixed sign for all sufficiently
large $k$. Consequently $\sum_{k\leq m}f(kq)$ grows linearly in
absolute value. This does not handle general nonperiodic sequences.
