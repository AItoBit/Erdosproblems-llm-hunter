\section*{Problem 376. Infinitely many $n$ with $\gcd\!\bigl(\binom{2n}{n},105\bigr)=1$? --- Round 2}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}
Path (C): isolate a fatal obstruction in the strongest currently available fractal-intersection strategy, and correct an overoptimistic reading of the literature.

Round 1 already gave the exact Kummer digit reformulation and the unconditional two-prime theorem.
What remained unclear was whether recent fractal technology gives an \emph{unconditional} power-saving upper bound for the specific triple $(3,5,7)$.
In this round I show that the answer is \emph{no}: the existing method needs an extra linear-independence input which is not currently known for $(3,5,7)$.

This is strict progress because it identifies the exact missing hypothesis needed to push current methods onto Problem~376.

\subsection*{2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}
I use the following Round-1 facts.
\begin{itemize}
\item By Kummer's theorem,
\[
\gcd\!\bigl(\binom{2n}{n},105\bigr)=1
\]
if and only if all base-$3$ digits of $n$ lie in $\{0,1\}$, all base-$5$ digits lie in $\{0,1,2\}$, and all base-$7$ digits lie in $\{0,1,2,3\}$.
\item Erd\H{o}s--Graham--Ruzsa--Straus proved infinitude for any \emph{two} odd primes.
\item Round 1 recorded that the three-prime problem for $105$ remains open.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}
The new tool is a careful reading of Burrell--Yu's three-base intersection theorem.

Their method does give a power-saving upper bound for the counting function
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N):=\#\Bigl\{n\le N:\gcd\!\bigl(\binom{2n}{n},105\bigr)=1\Bigr\},
\]
but only under the extra hypothesis that
\[
1,\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 5},\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 7}
\]
are linearly independent over the rationals (equivalently, that $(3,5,7)$ is strongly multiplicatively independent in their terminology).

Unconditionally, they prove only that \emph{at least one} of the triples
\[
(3,5,7),\qquad (3,5,11),\qquad (3,5,13)
\]
has the required independence property.
That existential statement is not enough to say anything unconditional about $105$ itself.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}
The exact gap after Round 1 is this:

\medskip

\centerline{\emph{Can one apply current three-Cantor-set intersection theorems directly to the specific triple $(3,5,7)$?}}

\medskip

I will answer this in two steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item State precisely the extra hypothesis Burrell--Yu need in order to get a power-saving upper bound for $(3,5,7)$.
\item Show that their unconditional theorem is only existential in the third prime $q\in\{7,11,13\}$, so it cannot be specialized to $q=7$.
\end{enumerate}
This isolates the exact obstruction in the present proof strategy.

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

\subsubsection*{Proposition 5.1 (conditional power-saving for Problem~376)}
Assume that
\[
1,\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 5},\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 7}
\]
are linearly independent over the rationals.
Then
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N)\ll N^{\theta_{3,5,7}+o(1)},
\qquad
\theta_{3,5,7}:=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}+\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}+\frac{\log 4}{\log 7}-2.
\]
In particular,
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N)\ll N^{0.026}
\]
for all sufficiently large $N$.

\paragraph{Justification.}
Burrell--Yu's three-base intersection theorem applies to digit-restricted sets in strongly multiplicatively independent bases.
For Problem~376 the allowed digit counts are
\[
2 \text{ in base }3,\qquad 3 \text{ in base }5,\qquad 4 \text{ in base }7.
\]
Hence the relevant dimension sum is
\[
\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}+\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}+\frac{\log 4}{\log 7},
\]
and their theorem yields an upper bound of exponent equal to this quantity minus $2$.
Numerically,
\[
\theta_{3,5,7}\approx 0.0259<0.026.
\]
So under the displayed linear-independence hypothesis one gets
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N)\ll N^{0.026}.
\]
Under Schanuel's conjecture this linear independence is available, so the same conditional upper bound follows.

\subsubsection*{Proposition 5.2 (the exact unconditional obstruction)}
Burrell--Yu's unconditional argument does \emph{not} imply any power-saving upper bound for the specific set $G_{3,5,7}(N)$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Their notion of strong multiplicative independence for three bases $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$ is exactly the linear independence over the rationals of
\[
1,\ \frac{\log p_1}{\log p_2},\ \frac{\log p_1}{\log p_3}.
\]
For the triple $(3,5,7)$ this becomes
\[
1,\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 5},\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 7}.
\]
Burrell--Yu do \emph{not} prove this unconditionally.

What they do prove unconditionally is weaker: by a six-exponentials argument they show that at least one of
\[
(3,5,7),\qquad (3,5,11),\qquad (3,5,13)
\]
is strongly multiplicatively independent.
Consequently, one gets a power-saving upper bound for at least one of the three counting functions
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N),\qquad G_{3,5,11}(N),\qquad G_{3,5,13}(N),
\]
but not necessarily for the first one.

In the worst case the independent triple is only $(3,5,13)$, whose exponent is
\[
\theta_{3,5,13}
=
\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}+\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}+\frac{\log 7}{\log 13}-2
\approx 0.0722<0.073.
\]
Thus the unconditional output of that paper is merely:
\[
\text{for at least one } q\in\{7,11,13\},\quad G_{3,5,q}(N)\ll N^{0.073},
\]
and this gives no unconditional upper bound for Problem~376 itself.

\subsubsection*{Corollary 5.3 (a sharply formulated obstruction)}
The present three-fractal strategy would settle the \emph{upper-bound} side of Problem~376 if one could prove
\[
1,\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 5},\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 7}
\]
are linearly independent over the rationals.
At present this is the exact missing hypothesis blocking that route.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
Potential failure modes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Did I accidentally turn an existential theorem into a theorem for $q=7$?}  
No.  This is precisely what I ruled out.  The unconditional theorem says ``for at least one $q\in\{7,11,13\}$'', not ``for each such $q$''.

\item \emph{Is the exponent $0.026$ unconditional for $105$?}  
No.  In the argument above it is explicitly conditional on the displayed linear independence (for example via Schanuel).

\item \emph{Does this settle the original infinitude problem?}  
No.  Even a power-saving upper bound still allows infinitely many solutions.  This round isolates an obstruction in one proof strategy; it does not prove finiteness or infinitude.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{7) FINAL}
\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

The strict Round-2 advance is a precise obstruction theorem:
current three-base fractal methods do \emph{not} give an unconditional upper bound for the specific problem with $105$.
They would yield
\[
G_{3,5,7}(N)\ll N^{0.026}
\]
under the extra hypothesis that
\[
1,\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 5},\ \frac{\log 3}{\log 7}
\]
are linearly independent over the rationals, but unconditionally they prove only that one of the triples $(3,5,7)$, $(3,5,11)$, $(3,5,13)$ enjoys a power-saving upper bound.
So the exact missing ingredient for this route is now sharply identified.
