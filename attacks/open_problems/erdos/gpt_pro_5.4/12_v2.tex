\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem \#12 --- Round 3 (Continuation \& Gap-Closure Mode)}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-3 OBJECTIVE}

\textbf{Path (C): obstruction/correction inside the Round-2 square-based program.}

Round~2 proved that whenever
\[
S\subseteq \mathbb N_{\ge 2}
\]
is primitive and every prime factor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$, the square set
\[
A(S):=\{s^2:s\in S\}
\]
satisfies~(\*). That opened a natural constructive program: make $S$ as large as possible.

Round~2, however, only supplied fixed-$k$ squarefree $k$-prime examples (Corollary~12.4b and Proposition~12.5), giving
\[
|A_k\cap[1,N]|\asymp \frac{\sqrt N}{\log N}(\log\log N)^{k-1},
\]
and it only ruled out one special ``private prime'' strategy.

The most promising Round-3 task is therefore to settle the \emph{internal size question} for the whole Round-2 square-based class:
\begin{quote}
How large can a primitive set $S$ supported on primes $3\bmod 4$ actually be?
\end{quote}
I will prove a sharp answer (for infinitely many scales): inside this entire class, one can reach
\[
\gg \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}
\]
along an infinite sequence of $N$, but one can never do better than
\[
\ll \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}
\]
in general. Thus the Round-2 square-based strategy has an intrinsic $\sqrt{\log N}$ barrier and cannot by itself settle (Q1).

\subsection*{2) ROUND-2 FOUNDATION USED}

I rely on the following specific Round~2 results.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Round-2 Lemma 12.3 / Corollary 12.3a:} for $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$,
\[
v_p(x^2+y^2)=2\min(v_p(x),v_p(y)),
\]
hence $p^{2e}\mid (x^2+y^2)$ forces $p^e\mid x,y$.

\item \textbf{Round-2 Theorem 12.4:} if $S$ is primitive and every prime divisor of every element of $S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$, then
\[
A(S)=\{s^2:s\in S\}
\]
satisfies property~(\*).

\item \textbf{Round-2 Proposition 12.6:} a certain ``private prime'' subclass cannot achieve $\gg \sqrt N$.
Round~3 replaces that by an obstruction for the \emph{entire} square-based class coming from Theorem~12.4.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-3)}

The genuinely new ingredient is a \textbf{block-antichain construction} inside the multiplicative semigroup
\[
\mathcal M:=\{n\in\mathbb N:\ p\mid n\Rightarrow p\equiv 3\pmod 4\}.
\]

There are two parts.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Analytic size of $\mathcal M$.}
By a standard Selberg--Delange theorem (specialised below),
\[
M(x):=|\mathcal M\cap[1,x]|\asymp \frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.
\]

\item \textbf{Primitive extraction by thick blocks.}
Although $\mathcal M$ itself is not primitive, a subset of one interval $(x/3,x]$ \emph{is} primitive, because the least nontrivial element of $\mathcal M$ is $3$.
By choosing a very sparse sequence of scales $x_j$ and taking many elements of $\mathcal M\cap(x_j/3,x_j]$ that avoid divisibility by earlier chosen elements, one can inductively build an infinite primitive
\[
S\subseteq \mathcal M
\]
with
\[
|S\cap[1,x_j]|\gg \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
\]
for infinitely many $j$.
\end{enumerate}

This is a strict strengthening of Round~2's fixed-$k$ families, and it also shows that the entire Round-2 square-based method has a built-in $\sqrt{\log N}$ loss.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-3)}

\textbf{Exact Round-2 gap.}
Round~2 showed that \emph{some} primitive sets $S\subseteq \mathcal M$ work, but did not determine the true order of magnitude obtainable from this paradigm.

\textbf{Claims to prove now.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove an analytic counting estimate for $\mathcal M$:
\[
M(x)\asymp \frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.
\]

\item Deduce an \emph{upper bound} for the whole Round-2 square-based class:
for every primitive $S\subseteq \mathcal M$,
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N]|\ll \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}.
\]
Hence no set produced via Round-2 Theorem~12.4 can answer (Q1).

\item Construct an infinite primitive $S\subseteq \mathcal M$ with
\[
|S\cap[1,x_j]|\gg \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
\]
for infinitely many $x_j$, and then invoke Round-2 Theorem~12.4 to obtain
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N_j]|\gg \frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{\sqrt{\log N_j}}
\qquad\text{for infinitely many }N_j=x_j^2.
\]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why this overcomes the previous obstacle.}
Round~2 used only thin fixed-$k$ layers of $\mathcal M$. The new construction instead uses \emph{full local density} of $\mathcal M$ on carefully separated scales, while preserving primitivity by:
\begin{itemize}
\item taking each block inside an interval of ratio $<3$, and
\item excluding multiples of earlier chosen elements.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-3)}

\paragraph{\textbf{Notation.}}
Let
\[
\mathcal M:=\{n\in\mathbb N:\ p\mid n\Longrightarrow p\equiv 3\pmod 4\},
\qquad
M(x):=|\mathcal M\cap[1,x]|.
\]
Note that $1\in \mathcal M$, and the least element of $\mathcal M\setminus\{1\}$ is $3$.

\bigskip

\paragraph{\textbf{Proposition 12.7 (analytic counting input for $\mathcal M$).}}
There exists a constant $C_{\mathcal M}>0$ such that
\[
M(x)=C_{\mathcal M}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+O\!\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}}\right)
\qquad (x\to\infty).
\]
Consequently there exist positive constants $c_1,c_2,x_0$ such that for all $x\ge x_0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:M-upper}
M(x)\le c_2\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:M-interval}
M(x)-M(x/3)\ge c_1\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.
\end{equation}

\emph{Proof.}
Apply the Selberg--Delange asymptotic of Chang--Martin, Theorem~3.4, with modulus $q=4$ and residue class set $B=\{3\}$.
Then $N_B$ in their notation is exactly the set $\mathcal M$ above, and their exponent is
\[
\beta=\frac{|B|}{\varphi(4)}=\frac12.
\]
Hence
\[
M(x)=\#\{n\le x:\ n\in\mathcal M\}
=
\frac{G_B(1)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}
+O\!\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}}\right),
\]
which is the asserted formula with
\[
C_{\mathcal M}=\frac{G_B(1)}{\Gamma(1/2)}>0.
\]
The upper bound \eqref{eq:M-upper} is immediate.

For \eqref{eq:M-interval}, subtract the same asymptotic at $x$ and at $x/3$:
\[
M(x)-M(x/3)
=
C_{\mathcal M}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}
-
C_{\mathcal M}\frac{x/3}{\sqrt{\log(x/3)}}
+
O\!\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}}\right).
\]
Since
\[
\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\sqrt{\log(x/3)}}\to 1
\qquad (x\to\infty),
\]
the main term equals
\[
\left(\frac23+o(1)\right)C_{\mathcal M}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}},
\]
which is positive for all sufficiently large $x$.
After reducing $c_1$ if necessary and enlarging $x_0$, \eqref{eq:M-interval} follows.
\qed

\bigskip

\paragraph{\textbf{Proposition 12.8 (upper bound for the entire Round-2 square-based class).}}
Let $S\subseteq \mathcal M\setminus\{1\}$ be primitive, and set
\[
A(S):=\{s^2:\ s\in S\}.
\]
Then for all large $N$,
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N]|
=
|S\cap[1,\sqrt N]|
\ll
\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}.
\]
In particular,
\[
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A(S)\cap[1,N]|}{\sqrt N}=0.
\]
So no set obtained from Round-2 Theorem~12.4 can solve \textnormal{(Q1)}.

\emph{Proof.}
Because $S\subseteq \mathcal M$,
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N]|=|S\cap[1,\sqrt N]|\le M(\sqrt N).
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:M-upper},
\[
M(\sqrt N)\le c_2\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log(\sqrt N)}}
=
\sqrt 2\,c_2\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}},
\]
which proves the claim.
\qed

\bigskip

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 12.9 (block-antichain construction inside $\mathcal M$).}}
There exists an infinite primitive set
\[
S\subseteq \mathcal M\setminus\{1\}
\]
such that for infinitely many $x$,
\[
|S\cap[1,x]|\gg \frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Fix constants $c_1,c_2,x_0$ as in Proposition~12.7, and set
\[
\eta:=\frac{c_1}{32c_2}.
\]
We will inductively construct:

\begin{itemize}
\item a strictly increasing sequence $x_1<x_2<\cdots$ tending to infinity,
\item finite sets $T_j\subseteq \mathcal M\cap (x_j/3,x_j]$,
\end{itemize}
such that if
\[
S_j:=T_1\cup\cdots\cup T_j,
\]
then
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $S_j$ is primitive;
\item[(b)] $\displaystyle \sum_{s\in S_j}\frac1s\le \eta\sum_{i=1}^j 2^{-i}<\eta$;
\item[(c)] $\displaystyle |T_j|\ge \frac{c_1}{5}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}$.
\end{enumerate}

Once this is done, the infinite union
\[
S:=\bigcup_{j\ge 1}T_j
\]
is primitive, and for each $j$ we have
\[
|S\cap[1,x_j]|\ge |T_j|\ge \frac{c_1}{5}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}},
\]
which proves the theorem.

\medskip

\emph{Induction step.}
Suppose $S_{j-1}$ has already been constructed (for $j=1$, take $S_0=\varnothing$).
Choose $x_j$ so large that all of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $x_j\ge x_0$;
\item[(ii)] $x_j>3x_{j-1}$ (for $j=1$ there is no restriction);
\item[(iii)] $x_j>(\max S_{j-1})^2$ (vacuous if $S_{j-1}=\varnothing$);
\item[(iv)] $\displaystyle \frac{3c_1}{4\sqrt{\log x_j}}\le \frac{\eta}{2^j}$;
\item[(v)] $\displaystyle \frac{c_1}{4}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}\ge 1$.
\end{enumerate}
This is possible because $x_j$ may be taken arbitrarily large.

Now consider the interval
\[
I_j:=(x_j/3,x_j].
\]
By \eqref{eq:M-interval},
\[
|\mathcal M\cap I_j|
=
M(x_j)-M(x_j/3)
\ge c_1\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}.
\]
Let $E_j$ be the set of elements of $\mathcal M\cap I_j$ divisible by at least one element of $S_{j-1}$.
Then by the union bound,
\[
|E_j|
\le \sum_{s\in S_{j-1}}\#\{n\in\mathcal M\cap I_j:\ s\mid n\}.
\]
If $s\mid n$ and $n\in \mathcal M$, then $n=sm$ with $m\in\mathcal M$.
Hence
\[
\#\{n\in\mathcal M\cap I_j:\ s\mid n\}\le M(x_j/s).
\]
Therefore
\[
|E_j|
\le \sum_{s\in S_{j-1}} M(x_j/s).
\]
Because $s\le \max S_{j-1}<\sqrt{x_j}$ by (iii), we have $x_j/s>\sqrt{x_j}$ and so
\[
\log(x_j/s)\ge \frac12\log x_j.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:M-upper},
\[
M(x_j/s)\le c_2\frac{x_j/s}{\sqrt{\log(x_j/s)}}
\le \sqrt 2\,c_2\frac{x_j}{s\sqrt{\log x_j}}
\le 2c_2\frac{x_j}{s\sqrt{\log x_j}}.
\]
Summing over $s\in S_{j-1}$ and using the induction hypothesis~(b),
\[
|E_j|
\le 2c_2\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}\sum_{s\in S_{j-1}}\frac1s
< 2c_2\eta\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
=
\frac{c_1}{16}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}.
\]
Thus the set
\[
U_j:=(\mathcal M\cap I_j)\setminus E_j
\]
of admissible new elements satisfies
\[
|U_j|
\ge \left(c_1-\frac{c_1}{16}\right)\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
=
\frac{15c_1}{16}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}.
\]
Choose any subset
\[
T_j\subseteq U_j
\]
of cardinality
\[
m_j:=\left\lfloor \frac{c_1}{4}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}\right\rfloor.
\]
This is possible because $|U_j|>m_j$ for large $x_j$, and by (v) we have $m_j\ge 1$.
Moreover
\[
m_j\ge \frac{c_1}{5}\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
\]
for all sufficiently large $x_j$, yielding~(c).

Next, because every element of $T_j$ is $>x_j/3$, its reciprocal is at most $3/x_j$, so
\[
\sum_{t\in T_j}\frac1t
\le \frac{3m_j}{x_j}
\le \frac{3c_1}{4\sqrt{\log x_j}}
\le \frac{\eta}{2^j}
\]
by (iv). Hence
\[
\sum_{s\in S_j}\frac1s
=
\sum_{s\in S_{j-1}}\frac1s+\sum_{t\in T_j}\frac1t
\le \eta\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}2^{-i}+\frac{\eta}{2^j}
=
\eta\sum_{i=1}^{j}2^{-i},
\]
so the induction hypothesis~(b) is preserved.

Finally we verify primitivity.
\begin{itemize}
\item No old element divides a new element: this is exactly how $U_j$ was defined.
\item No new element divides an old element: by (ii), every new element exceeds $x_j/3>x_{j-1}$, while every old element is at most $x_{j-1}$.
\item No two distinct new elements divide one another: if $u,v\in T_j$ with $u<v$ and $u\mid v$, then
\[
v/u\in \mathcal M\setminus\{1\},
\]
so $v/u\ge 3$. But since $u>x_j/3$ and $v\le x_j$,
\[
v/u<3,
\]
a contradiction.
\end{itemize}
Thus $S_j$ is primitive, completing the induction and the proof.
\qed

\bigskip

\paragraph{\textbf{Corollary 12.9a (new valid construction for the original divisibility problem).}}
There exists an infinite set $A\subseteq \mathbb N$ satisfying~(\*) such that for infinitely many $N$,
\[
|A\cap[1,N]|\gg \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Let $S$ be as in Theorem~12.9, and define
\[
A:=A(S)=\{s^2:\ s\in S\}.
\]
Since $S$ is primitive and every prime factor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$, Round-2 Theorem~12.4 implies that $A$ satisfies~(\*).

Now take $N_j:=x_j^2$. Then
\[
|A\cap[1,N_j]|
=
|S\cap[1,x_j]|
\gg
\frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}
=
\sqrt 2\,\frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{\sqrt{\log N_j}}.
\]
Hence the claimed lower bound holds for infinitely many $N=N_j$.
\qed

\bigskip

\paragraph{\textbf{Theorem 12.10 (sharp size scale inside the Round-2 square-based class).}}
Among all sets of the form
\[
A(S)=\{s^2:\ s\in S\},
\]
where $S\subseteq \mathcal M\setminus\{1\}$ is primitive, the correct order of magnitude along infinitely many scales is
\[
\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}.
\]
More precisely:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every such $A(S)$ satisfies
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N]|\ll \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}
\qquad (N\to\infty),
\]
by Proposition~12.8;
\item there exists such an $A(S)$ with
\[
|A(S)\cap[1,N_j]|\gg \frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{\sqrt{\log N_j}}
\]
for infinitely many $N_j$, by Corollary~12.9a.
\end{enumerate}

Thus the full Round-2 square-based construction method has now been pushed to its natural asymptotic limit (up to constants) on an infinite subsequence.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

\textbf{(i) Does the block construction really preserve primitivity?}
Yes. Inside one block $(x_j/3,x_j]$, divisibility would force a quotient in $\mathcal M\setminus\{1\}$, hence at least $3$, but every quotient of two numbers in that interval is $<3$.
Across different blocks, earlier elements are explicitly forbidden from dividing later ones, and later elements are too large to divide earlier ones.

\textbf{(ii) Is the estimate for excluded multiples legitimate?}
Yes. If $s\in S_{j-1}$ divides $n\in\mathcal M$, then $n=sm$ with $m\in\mathcal M$.
Thus the number of multiples of $s$ up to $x_j$ is at most $M(x_j/s)$.
No independence assumption is used; only the union bound.

\textbf{(iii) Did the proof accidentally use more than finite reciprocal mass of old elements?}
No. The key inequality is
\[
|E_j|\ll \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{\log x_j}}\sum_{s\in S_{j-1}}\frac1s.
\]
Only the \emph{finite} reciprocal sum of the previously chosen blocks enters.
The construction deliberately makes the blockwise reciprocal contributions summable.

\textbf{(iv) Is the analytic input applied correctly?}
Yes. Chang--Martin Theorem~3.4 counts integers all of whose prime factors lie in a fixed set of reduced residue classes.
With $q=4$ and the single class $3\bmod 4$, this is exactly the semigroup $\mathcal M$.
The exponent is $\beta=1/\varphi(4)=1/2$, giving the $\sqrt{\log x}$ denominator.

\textbf{(v) Does Corollary~12.9a settle (Q1)?}
No. It only yields the lower bound
\[
|A\cap[1,N_j]|\gg \frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{\sqrt{\log N_j}}
\]
for infinitely many $N_j$.
This is \emph{far} from the required
\[
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,N]|}{\sqrt N}>0.
\]
So Q1 remains open.

\textbf{(vi) Does this contradict the density-zero theorem of Erd\H{o}s--S\'ark\"ozy?}
No. Both
\[
\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}=o(x)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}=o(\sqrt N).
\]
So the new construction is sparse enough to be consistent with all known upper bounds.

\textbf{(vii) Is the Round-2 theorem being used in exactly the permitted way?}
Yes. I do not re-prove Round-2 Theorem~12.4; I invoke it exactly once, at the end, after constructing a larger primitive $S$.
The new mathematical work is entirely the analytic counting of $\mathcal M$ plus the primitive extraction argument.

\subsection*{7) FINAL}

\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

Round~3 does \emph{not} solve the original Erd\H{o}s problem in full generality.
However, it gives a sharp obstruction/correction for the whole constructive framework introduced in Round~2:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Within the entire square-based class supplied by Round-2 Theorem~12.4, the best possible size scale is}
\[
|A\cap[1,N]|\asymp \frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\log N}}
\]
\emph{along infinitely many scales, and in particular this class can never realise the target in (Q1).}
\end{quote}

This is a substantive advance beyond Round~2 for two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
\item it replaces the fixed-$k$ families by a much denser valid construction, namely
\[
|A\cap[1,N_j]|\gg \frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{\sqrt{\log N_j}}
\]
for infinitely many $N_j$;
\item it proves that \emph{no} construction obtainable from Round-2 Theorem~12.4 can exceed this order by more than constants.
\end{enumerate}

So the next unresolved gap is now sharply isolated:

\begin{quote}
Any eventual positive solution to \textnormal{(Q1)} must leave the Round-2 square-based paradigm entirely (or augment it with new structure not covered by primitive subsets of $\mathcal M$).
\end{quote}
