\section{Erd\H{o}s Problem \#203}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}

\textbf{Path pursued: (C)-style obstruction sharpening / strict advance without a full resolution.}

Round~1 proved a finite-box lemma: for any fixed finite set of exponent pairs $(k,\ell)$, one can choose $m$ so that all corresponding values $2^k3^\ell m+1$ are composite. The obvious gap was that this did not control \emph{infinitely many} exponent pairs.

The most promising Round~2 move is therefore to prove a strictly stronger theorem that kills \emph{infinitely many exponent pairs at once} in a structured way, and then identify the precise remaining obstruction.

The new goal in this round is to pass from ``finite boxes'' to ``finite collections of infinite rays'' in the exponent lattice.

\subsection*{2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}

I use the following Round~1 items.

\begin{itemize}
\item The formal restatement of the problem.
\item The Round~1 finite-box lemma: any \emph{finite} set of exponent pairs $(k,\ell)$ can be forced composite simultaneously by a CRT construction.
\item The Round~1 observation that for odd $m>1$, the value at $(k,\ell)=(0,0)$ is
\[
m+1,
\]
which is even and at least $4$, hence automatically composite.
\end{itemize}

I do not reprove the finite-box lemma. It is used only as the comparison point that Round~2 must beat.

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}

The new ingredient is a theorem of Filaseta on \emph{generalized Sierpi\'nski numbers}:

\medskip

\emph{For every fixed integer $A\ge 2$, there exists an arithmetic progression of odd positive integers $m$ such that}
\[
m a^n+1
\]
\emph{is composite for every integer $a$ with $2\le a\le A$ and every $n\ge 1$.}

\medskip

This is dramatically stronger than the Round~1 finite-box lemma. It controls infinitely many exponents $n$ simultaneously for \emph{every base} $a$ in a fixed finite interval.

The exponent pairs $(k,\ell)$ for the original problem naturally decompose into rays:
\[
(k,\ell)=n(u,v),
\]
where $(u,v)$ is primitive, meaning $\gcd(u,v)=1$, and $n\ge 1$.

Thus Filaseta's theorem lets us kill every pair on any fixed finite collection of primitive rays, because each ray corresponds to the fixed base
\[
a=2^u3^v.
\]

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}

The exact Round~1 gap was:
\[
\text{finite sets of exponent pairs are controllable, but infinite structured families were not.}
\]

To overcome this, I will prove:

\begin{enumerate}
\item a \emph{finite-ray annihilation theorem}: for any finite set of directions $(u,v)$, there exists an arithmetic progression of odd integers $m$ such that
\[
2^{nu}3^{nv}m+1
\]
is composite for all $n\ge 1$ and for every chosen direction $(u,v)$;

\item an \emph{infinite-prime strengthening}: for any such finite ray set, there are infinitely many \emph{prime} choices of $m$ with the same property;

\item a precise reformulation of the remaining obstruction: the original problem is equivalent to asking whether one can annihilate \emph{all primitive rays simultaneously}.
\end{enumerate}

This goes well beyond Round~1, which only handled finitely many isolated lattice points.

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

\paragraph{External theorem used (Filaseta).}
Fix an integer $A\ge 2$. Then there exists an arithmetic progression of odd positive integers $m$ such that
\[
m a^n+1
\]
is composite for every integer $a$ with $2\le a\le A$ and every integer $n\ge 1$.

\paragraph{External corollary used (Filaseta + Maynard/Shiu).}
For the same fixed $A$, let $K(A)$ denote the set of odd positive integers $m$ such that
\[
m a^n+1 \quad \text{and} \quad m+a^n
\]
are composite for all integers $a$ with $2\le a\le A$ and all $n\ge 1$. Then $K(A)$ contains infinitely many primes; in fact, Filaseta records arbitrarily long strings of consecutive primes in $K(A)$.

\paragraph{Theorem 5.1 (finite-ray annihilation).}
Let
\[
D\subset \{(u,v)\in \mathbf{Z}_{\ge 0}^2:(u,v)\neq (0,0)\}
\]
be finite. Then there exists an arithmetic progression of odd positive integers $m$ such that
\[
2^{nu}3^{nv}m+1
\]
is composite for every $(u,v)\in D$ and every integer $n\ge 1$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Set
\[
A:=\max\{2^u3^v:(u,v)\in D\}.
\]
Since $D$ is finite, $A$ is a finite positive integer, and because $(u,v)\neq (0,0)$ for every $(u,v)\in D$, we have $2^u3^v\ge 2$, so $A\ge 2$.

Apply Filaseta's theorem to this $A$. Then there exists an arithmetic progression of odd positive integers $m$ such that
\[
m a^n+1
\]
is composite for every integer $a$ with $2\le a\le A$ and every $n\ge 1$.

Now fix $(u,v)\in D$. The number
\[
a:=2^u3^v
\]
satisfies $2\le a\le A$. Therefore, for every $n\ge 1$,
\[
m a^n+1 = m(2^u3^v)^n+1 = m\,2^{nu}3^{nv}+1
\]
is composite.

Since this holds for every $(u,v)\in D$, the theorem follows.
\hfill $\square$

\paragraph{Corollary 5.2 (finite-ray annihilation by infinitely many primes).}
With $D$ as above, there are infinitely many \emph{prime} numbers $m$ such that
\[
2^{nu}3^{nv}m+1
\]
is composite for every $(u,v)\in D$ and every $n\ge 1$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Choose $A$ as in Theorem~5.1. By the cited corollary in Filaseta's paper, the set $K(A)$ contains infinitely many primes. Every $m\in K(A)$ satisfies
\[
m a^n+1 \text{ composite for all } 2\le a\le A,\ n\ge 1.
\]
In particular this holds for the finitely many bases $a=2^u3^v$ with $(u,v)\in D$. Hence every prime $m\in K(A)$ has the desired finite-ray annihilation property.
\hfill $\square$

\paragraph{Proposition 5.3 (primitive-ray decomposition).}
Every exponent pair $(k,\ell)\in \mathbf{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\setminus \{(0,0)\}$ can be written uniquely in the form
\[
(k,\ell)=n(u,v),
\]
where $n\ge 1$ and $(u,v)\in \mathbf{Z}_{\ge 0}^2$ is primitive, i.e.
\[
\gcd(u,v)=1.
\]

\paragraph{Proof.}
Let
\[
n:=\gcd(k,\ell),
\]
with the conventions $\gcd(k,0)=k$ and $\gcd(0,\ell)=\ell$. Since $(k,\ell)\neq (0,0)$, we have $n\ge 1$. Define
\[
u:=k/n,\qquad v:=\ell/n.
\]
Then $(u,v)\in \mathbf{Z}_{\ge 0}^2$, $(k,\ell)=n(u,v)$, and $\gcd(u,v)=1$ by construction.

For uniqueness, suppose
\[
(k,\ell)=n(u,v)=n'(u',v')
\]
with both $(u,v)$ and $(u',v')$ primitive. Taking gcds of the coordinates on both sides gives $n=n'$, and then $(u,v)=(u',v')$ follows immediately.
\hfill $\square$

\paragraph{Sharp reformulation of the remaining obstruction.}
By Proposition~5.3, the original problem asks whether there exists an odd $m>1$ such that every value
\[
2^k3^\ell m+1
\]
is composite, equivalently: whether one can choose a single $m$ that annihilates \emph{every primitive ray}
\[
\{n(u,v):n\ge 1\}, \qquad \gcd(u,v)=1.
\]

Theorem~5.1 and Corollary~5.2 show that \emph{every finite collection of primitive rays} can be annihilated simultaneously, even by infinitely many prime choices of $m$.

So the unresolved step is now isolated sharply:

\medskip

\emph{Can one pass from finite primitive-ray annihilation to annihilation of the full infinite primitive-ray set?}

\medskip

This is strictly stronger than the Round~1 finite-box perspective, because each ray contains infinitely many exponent pairs.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

I now check the places where the new argument could fail.

\paragraph{(i) Does Theorem~5.1 accidentally miss the pair $(0,0)$?}
Yes, it misses it intentionally, because the ray decomposition only applies to nonzero exponent pairs. But for odd $m>1$,
\[
2^0 3^0 m +1 = m+1
\]
is even and at least $4$, hence composite. So this is not a real gap.

\paragraph{(ii) Are the axis directions included?}
Yes. The primitive rays $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are included in the framework and correspond to the bases $2$ and $3$.

\paragraph{(iii) Is it legitimate to choose $A$ as the maximum of the bases $2^u3^v$?}
Yes. Filaseta's theorem works for \emph{all} integers $a$ with $2\le a\le A$, so it certainly covers the special finitely many integers of the form $2^u3^v$ that I need.

\paragraph{(iv) Does the prime strengthening really follow?}
Yes. Filaseta's Corollary~4.4 gives infinitely many primes in $K(A)$, and every such prime belongs to the annihilating set from Theorem~5.1.

\paragraph{(v) Have I solved the original problem?}
No. The argument only proves finite-ray annihilation, not annihilation of the full infinite primitive-ray family. That remaining passage is exactly the unresolved step, and I have stated it explicitly.

So the Round~2 work is rigorous and strictly stronger than Round~1, but it still falls short of a full resolution.

\subsection*{7) FINAL (EXACTLY ONE)}

\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

The strict Round~2 advance is the finite-ray annihilation theorem: any finite collection of primitive rays in the $(k,\ell)$-lattice can be annihilated simultaneously, even by infinitely many prime choices of $m$.

This is substantially stronger than the Round~1 finite-box lemma and isolates the exact remaining obstruction: extending from finitely many primitive rays to all primitive rays.
