\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}

There are two new points.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The current problem page is \emph{not self-consistent if read literally}.  Its displayed statement asks only for an infinite composite Fibonacci-like sequence with no common divisor $>1$, but the page text itself says that Graham already proved the existence of such a sequence, and Round-1 re-proved this with a much smaller example.
\item The comments show that the intended stronger question is really about excluding a \emph{finite prime / covering-congruence explanation} of compositeness, at least for the odd-indexed terms in the Ismailescu--Son construction.
\end{enumerate}

The key Round-2 tool is the following formalization of that comment-based obstruction.

\medskip
\centerline{\emph{Odd-subsequence finite-prime obstruction}}
\[
(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})\qquad
\forall m>1\ \exists n\ge 0\ \mbox{such that}\ \gcd(m,x_{2n+1})=1.
\]

This says exactly that the odd-indexed terms do \emph{not} admit any finite set of primes hitting every odd term.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}

The exact gap after Round-1 was that the formal existence statement had already been settled, but the comment-based strengthened question had not been isolated precisely.

I will now:

\begin{enumerate}
\item prove that the displayed statement is already solved and therefore cannot be the genuine remaining issue;
\item prove that $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$ is equivalent to ``the odd-indexed terms have no finite prime cover'';
\item prove that any Graham-type covering-congruence explanation of compositeness would force such a finite prime cover, so $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$ is a sufficient sharp obstruction;
\item explain exactly where Ismailescu--Son stop: they prove compositeness of all terms, but they do \emph{not} prove $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

\paragraph{Step 1: the displayed statement is already solved.}
Round-1 already proved the displayed formal statement by exhibiting the Vsemirnov pair
\[
x_0=106276436867,\qquad x_1=35256392432
\]
and checking that the resulting sequence has all terms composite and has no common divisor greater than $1$.

So the literal statement on the current page is not the real unresolved issue.

\paragraph{Step 2: a precise formulation of the comment-based strengthening.}
Define:

\medskip
\centerline{\emph{The odd terms have a finite prime cover}}
\medskip

if there exists a finite set of primes $P$ such that for every $n\ge 0$ there is some $p\in P$ with
\[
p\mid x_{2n+1}.
\]

\paragraph{Claim 1.}
For any integer sequence $(x_n)$, the following are equivalent:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] the odd terms have a finite prime cover;
\item[(ii)] there exists an integer $m>1$ such that
\[
\gcd(m,x_{2n+1})>1 \qquad \mbox{for every }n\ge 0.
\]
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Proof.}
If (i) holds, let $m$ be the product of the distinct primes in $P$.  Then every odd term $x_{2n+1}$ shares at least one prime factor with $m$, so $\gcd(m,x_{2n+1})>1$ for all $n$.

Conversely, if (ii) holds and $P$ is the finite set of prime divisors of $m$, then for each $n$ some prime $p\in P$ divides $\gcd(m,x_{2n+1})$, hence divides $x_{2n+1}$.  So the odd terms have a finite prime cover.  This proves the equivalence.

Therefore $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$ is exactly the negation of ``the odd terms have a finite prime cover''.

\paragraph{Step 3: why finite prime covers really do produce covering congruences.}
Fix a prime $p$ and consider the state sequence modulo $p$,
\[
v_n=(x_n,x_{n+1}) \in (\mathbf Z/p\mathbf Z)^2.
\]
The recurrence gives
\[
v_{n+1}=T(v_n),\qquad T(a,b)=(b,a+b).
\]
The map $T$ is bijective modulo $p$ because it has inverse
\[
T^{-1}(a,b)=(b-a,a).
\]
Since $(\mathbf Z/p\mathbf Z)^2$ is finite, the orbit $(v_n)$ is purely periodic from $n=0$.

Hence, for each fixed prime $p$, the set
\[
S_p=\{n\ge 0:\ p\mid x_n\}
\]
is a finite union of residue classes modulo some period $\pi_p$.

It follows that the set
\[
S^{\mathrm{odd}}_p=\{n\ge 0:\ p\mid x_{2n+1}\}
\]
is also a finite union of residue classes modulo $\pi_p$ (or, after simplification, modulo a divisor of $\pi_p$).

Therefore, if the odd terms have a finite prime cover by primes in $P$, then the sets
\[
S^{\mathrm{odd}}_p \qquad (p\in P)
\]
together give a finite covering of the odd indices by congruence classes.

So a finite prime cover of the odd terms automatically yields an ``underlying covering system of congruences'' in the natural Graham-type sense.

\paragraph{Step 4: the Ismailescu--Son candidate and the exact unresolved point.}
Ismailescu and Son construct a Fibonacci-like sequence by taking
\[
x_0=p^2+q^2,\qquad x_1=2pq+q^2,
\]
with $p=1$ and an explicit $129$-digit integer $q$.

They prove two different compositeness mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}
\item every odd term factors explicitly as
\[
x_{2n+1}=(pF_n+qF_{n+1})(pL_n+qL_{n+1}),
\]
so all odd terms are composite;
\item every even term is divisible by one of finitely many primes coming from a finite partial covering system, so all even terms are composite.
\end{enumerate}

They also prove
\[
\gcd(x_0,x_1)=1.
\]

Thus they produce a bona fide composite Fibonacci-like sequence with no common divisor $>1$.

However, as the 2025 comments correctly emphasize, they do \emph{not} prove $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$.  Equivalently, they do not prove that the odd-indexed subsequence has no finite prime cover.

This is the exact unresolved obstruction left by the comments.

\paragraph{Step 5: why the stronger all-terms version is too strong.}
A natural but \emph{incorrect} sharpening would be
\[
\forall m>1\ \exists n\ge 0\ \mbox{such that}\ \gcd(m,x_n)=1.
\]
That is too strong for the Ismailescu--Son candidate, because their even terms were deliberately built to have a finite prime cover.  So the comments cannot be formalized using the whole sequence; they must focus on the odd terms (or on an equivalent weakening).

This identifies the minimal sharp formalization suggested by the comments.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

Possible failure modes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Is $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$ really the same as ``no finite prime cover''?}  
Yes, by Claim 1.
\item \emph{Does a finite prime cover really imply covering congruences?}  
Yes.  For each fixed prime, divisibility positions are periodic because the two-dimensional state recursion modulo $p$ is a bijection on a finite set.
\item \emph{Is $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$ the unique correct formalization of the comments?}  
No.  It is a \emph{minimal sharp formalization} of the exact issue highlighted in the comments: the unknown existence of an integer $m>1$ with $\gcd(m,x_{2n+1})>1$ for every $n$.
\item \emph{Have we accidentally weakened the problem too much?}  
No.  Any Graham-type finite covering explanation of the whole sequence would in particular force a finite prime cover of the odd terms, hence violate $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$.
\item \emph{Have we accidentally claimed Ismailescu--Son solved the comment-based strengthening?}  
No.  The whole point of the section is that they solve the compositeness problem but not the finite-prime-cover obstruction for the odd subsequence.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{7) FINAL}

\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).}

The displayed formal existence statement was already solved in Round-1 and in the literature.  After incorporating the 2025 comments, the real remaining issue is the sharper condition $(\star_{\mathrm{odd}})$: whether one can obtain a composite Fibonacci-like sequence whose odd-indexed subsequence has no finite prime cover, equivalently no finite Graham-type covering-congruence explanation.  That sharpened statement remains open to me.
