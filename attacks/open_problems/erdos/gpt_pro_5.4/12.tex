\section{Round 2}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}

I pursue path \textbf{(C)} on the natural square-based class generated by the Round-1 example.
More precisely, I prove an \emph{exact structural criterion} for sets of the form
\[
A_S:=\{s^2:s\in S\},
\]
where every prime factor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$.
This gives two strictly new consequences beyond Round 1:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a denser explicit construction than the prime-square family, namely semiprime-squares, with
\[
|A\cap[1,N]|\gg \frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N},
\]
\item a \emph{fatal obstruction} to the Round-1 square strategy for Q1: every set in this entire square-based class has size $o(\sqrt N)$, so no such construction can prove a positive answer to Q1.
\end{enumerate}
Globally, Q1--Q3 remain unresolved.

\subsection*{2) ROUND-1 FOUNDATION USED}

I use the following Round-1 ingredients without reproving them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lemma 12.1 (residue-class obstruction).} For fixed $a\in A$, among larger elements of $A$ one cannot occupy both residues $r$ and $-r\pmod a$. In particular, in residue class $0\pmod a$, there cannot be two distinct larger multiples of $a$.
\item \textbf{Lemma 12.2 (prime-square example).} The proof there uses the key modular fact that for a prime $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$, the congruence $x^2\equiv -1\pmod p$ has no solution.
\end{itemize}
The new work below strengthens the modular idea from Lemma 12.2 to an exact $p$-adic statement and then converts the residue-$0$ obstruction from Lemma 12.1 into a full characterization of the square-based class.

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}

The genuinely new tool is the following $p$-adic strengthening of the Round-1 congruence argument.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{New tool.}
If $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$ is prime, then for all integers $x,y$,
\[
\nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2\min\bigl(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\bigr).
\]
Equivalently: if $p^{2e}\mid x^2+y^2$, then $p^e\mid x$ and $p^e\mid y$.
\medskip

This yields an exact reduction for the whole natural class $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$:
\begin{quote}
$A_S$ satisfies $(*)$ \textbf{if and only if} every $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.
\end{quote}
Thus the square-based problem becomes a purely multiplicative divisibility problem on $S$.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}

The main gap after Round 1 was this: the prime-square construction is rigorous but too sparse to address Q1, and Round 1 had no mechanism either to enlarge it substantially or to prove that the whole strategy must fail.

The Round-2 plan is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
\item strengthen the local modular obstruction from primes to exact prime-power divisibility;
\item derive a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for all square families built from $3\pmod 4$ primes;
\item use that criterion in two opposite directions:
\begin{itemize}
\item construct a denser family than prime squares (semiprime-squares), and
\item prove that \emph{every} construction in this entire square-based class still has cardinality $o(\sqrt N)$, so this line of attack cannot settle Q1 positively.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}

\textbf{Notation.}
Let
\[
\mathcal P_3:=\{p\text{ prime}: p\equiv 3\pmod 4\}.
\]
For a set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, write
\[
A_S:=\{s^2:s\in S\}.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Lemma 12.3 ($p$-adic valuation of a sum of two squares for $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$).}
Let $p\in\mathcal P_3$ and let $x,y\in\mathbb Z$. Then
\[
\nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2\min\bigl(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\bigr).
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Set
\[
t:=\min\bigl(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\bigr).
\]
Write
\[
x=p^t x_1,\qquad y=p^t y_1,
\]
where at least one of $x_1,y_1$ is not divisible by $p$.
Then
\[
x^2+y^2=p^{2t}(x_1^2+y_1^2).
\]
So it suffices to show that $p\nmid x_1^2+y_1^2$.

If exactly one of $x_1,y_1$ is divisible by $p$, then clearly $x_1^2+y_1^2\not\equiv 0\pmod p$.
Thus the only remaining case is $p\nmid x_1y_1$ and
\[
x_1^2+y_1^2\equiv 0\pmod p.
\]
Then
\[
(x_1y_1^{-1})^2\equiv -1\pmod p,
\]
which is impossible because $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$ and hence $-1$ is not a quadratic residue modulo $p$.
Therefore $p\nmid x_1^2+y_1^2$, and so
\[
\nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2t=2\min\bigl(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\bigr).
\]
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Corollary 12.4 (divisibility forced by square-divisibility).}
Let $u,v,w\in\mathbb N$, and assume every prime divisor of $u$ lies in $\mathcal P_3$.
If
\[
u^2\mid (v^2+w^2),
\]
then
\[
u\mid v\qquad\text{and}\qquad u\mid w.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Write
\[
u=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}
\]
with each $p_i\equiv 3\pmod 4$.
From $u^2\mid v^2+w^2$ we obtain
\[
\nu_{p_i}(v^2+w^2)\ge 2e_i
\qquad (1\le i\le r).
\]
By Lemma 12.3,
\[
2\min\bigl(\nu_{p_i}(v),\nu_{p_i}(w)\bigr)=\nu_{p_i}(v^2+w^2)\ge 2e_i,
\]
so
\[
\min\bigl(\nu_{p_i}(v),\nu_{p_i}(w)\bigr)\ge e_i.
\]
Hence $p_i^{e_i}\mid v$ and $p_i^{e_i}\mid w$ for every $i$, therefore $u\mid v$ and $u\mid w$.
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Theorem 12.5 (exact criterion for the $3\pmod 4$ square family).}
Let $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, and assume every prime divisor of every $s\in S$ lies in $\mathcal P_3$.
Then the set
\[
A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}
\]
satisfies property $(*)$ if and only if every $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.

\emph{Proof.}

\emph{Necessity.}
Assume $A_S$ satisfies $(*)$.
Suppose, toward contradiction, that some $s\in S$ divides two distinct larger elements $t,u\in S$ with $t>s$ and $u>s$.
Then in $A_S$ the three elements
\[
a=s^2,\qquad b=t^2,\qquad c=u^2
\]
are distinct, satisfy $b>a$ and $c>a$, and also
\[
a=s^2\mid t^2+u^2=b+c,
\]
contradicting $(*)$.
Hence each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.

\emph{Sufficiency.}
Assume now that each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.
Suppose, toward contradiction, that $A_S$ fails $(*)$.
Then there exist distinct $a,b,c\in A_S$ with $b>a$, $c>a$, and
\[
a\mid (b+c).
\]
Write
\[
a=s^2,\qquad b=t^2,\qquad c=u^2
\]
with $s,t,u\in S$.
Since $b>a$ and $c>a$, we have $t>s$ and $u>s$.
Also
\[
s^2=a\mid (b+c)=t^2+u^2.
\]
By Corollary 12.4, this implies $s\mid t$ and $s\mid u$.
Thus $s$ divides two distinct larger elements of $S$, contradicting the hypothesis on $S$.
Therefore $A_S$ satisfies $(*)$.
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Corollary 12.6 (primitive-basis corollary).}
Let $S\subseteq\mathbb N$ be \emph{primitive} (that is, no element of $S$ divides another distinct element of $S$), and assume every prime divisor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$.
Then $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ satisfies $(*)$.

\emph{Proof.}
In a primitive set, no $s\in S$ divides any larger element of $S$, so certainly no $s\in S$ divides two larger elements.
Apply Theorem 12.5.
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Proposition 12.7 (a denser explicit construction than prime squares).}
Define
\[
S_2:=\{pq: p<q,\ p,q\in\mathcal P_3\}
\]
and
\[
A_2:=A_{S_2}=\{(pq)^2: p<q,\ p,q\in\mathcal P_3\}.
\]
Then $A_2$ satisfies $(*)$.

\emph{Proof.}
Every element of $S_2$ is squarefree with exactly two prime factors.
Hence if $m,n\in S_2$ and $m\mid n$, then $m=n$ by unique factorization.
So $S_2$ is primitive.
Now apply Corollary 12.6.
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Proposition 12.8 (quantitative size of the semiprime-square family).}
There exists $c>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $x$,
\[
|S_2\cap[1,x]|\ge c\,\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}.
\]
Consequently, there exists $c'>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $N$,
\[
|A_2\cap[1,N]|\ge c'\,\frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N}.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Let
\[
\pi_3(y):=|\{p\le y: p\in\mathcal P_3\}|.
\]
By the prime number theorem in the progression $3\pmod 4$, there exist constants $c_0,C_0>0$ and $y_0$ such that for all $y\ge y_0$,
\[
c_0\frac{y}{\log y}\le \pi_3(y)\le C_0\frac{y}{\log y}.
\]

Fix $x$ large.
For every prime $p\in\mathcal P_3$ with $p\le x^{1/3}$ and every prime $q\in\mathcal P_3$ with
\[
x^{1/3}<q\le \frac{x}{p},
\]
we have $p<q$ and $pq\le x$, so $pq\in S_2\cap[1,x]$.
Moreover, because $q>x^{1/3}\ge p$, each such semiprime is counted exactly once by its smaller prime $p$.
Therefore
\[
|S_2\cap[1,x]|
\ge
\sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}
\Bigl(\pi_3(x/p)-\pi_3(x^{1/3})\Bigr).
\]
For $p\le x^{1/3}$ we have $x/p\ge x^{2/3}$, so for large $x$,
\[
\pi_3(x/p)\ge c_0\frac{x/p}{\log(x/p)}\ge c_0\frac{x}{p\log x}.
\]
Also,
\[
\pi_3(x^{1/3})\le C_1\frac{x^{1/3}}{\log x}
\]
for some absolute $C_1$.
Hence
\[
|S_2\cap[1,x]|
\ge
c_0\frac{x}{\log x}
\sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p
-
C_1\frac{x^{1/3}}{\log x}\,\pi_3(x^{1/3}).
\]
Using again $\pi_3(x^{1/3})\ll x^{1/3}/\log x$, the error term is
\[
\ll \frac{x^{2/3}}{(\log x)^2}.
\]
So it remains to lower-bound the reciprocal prime sum.
By partial summation,
\[
\sum_{\substack{p\le y\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p
=
\frac{\pi_3(y)}{y}+
\int_{2}^{y}\frac{\pi_3(t)}{t^2}\,dt.
\]
Applying the lower bound $\pi_3(t)\ge c_0 t/\log t$ for $t\ge y_0$ gives, for large $y$,
\[
\sum_{\substack{p\le y\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p
\ge
c_2\log\log y
\]
for some $c_2>0$.
Taking $y=x^{1/3}$, we obtain
\[
\sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p
\ge c_3\log\log x
\]
for large $x$ (since $\log\log x^{1/3}=\log\log x+O(1)$).
Substituting into the previous estimate yields
\[
|S_2\cap[1,x]|
\ge c\,\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}
\]
for all sufficiently large $x$.

Finally,
\[
|A_2\cap[1,N]|=|S_2\cap[1,\sqrt N]|
\ge c'\,\frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N}
\]
for all sufficiently large $N$, after adjusting constants.
\qed

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Proposition 12.9 (fatal obstruction to the Round-1 square strategy for Q1).}
Let
\[
\mathcal T:=\{n\in\mathbb N: \text{every prime divisor of $n$ is congruent to }3\pmod 4\}.
\]
Then
\[
|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|=o(x).
\]
Consequently, for every set $S\subseteq\mathcal T$ one has
\[
|A_S\cap[1,N]|=o(\sqrt N).
\]
In particular, \emph{no} construction of the form $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ lying in $3\pmod 4$ can answer Q1 positively.

\emph{Proof.}
Fix $Y\ge 3$, and define
\[
M_Y:=2\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}p.
\]
Every $n\in\mathcal T$ is coprime to $M_Y$.
Hence modulo $M_Y$, such an $n$ can lie only in residue classes that are nonzero modulo $2$ and nonzero modulo every prime $p\le Y$ with $p\equiv 1\pmod 4$.
Therefore the number of admissible residue classes modulo $M_Y$ is exactly
\[
R_Y:=\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}(p-1),
\]
since modulo $2$ there is just one admissible class, namely $1$.
Thus, for every $x$,
\[
|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|
\le
R_Y\left(\frac{x}{M_Y}+1\right)
=
\frac{R_Y}{M_Y}x+R_Y.
\]
Now
\[
\frac{R_Y}{M_Y}
=
\frac12\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
\]
By the prime number theorem in the progression $1\pmod 4$, the reciprocal sum
\[
\sum_{\substack{p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\frac1p
\]
diverges. Hence
\[
\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\left(1-\frac1p\right)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad (Y\to\infty).
\]
So given $\varepsilon>0$, we may choose $Y$ so large that
\[
\frac{R_Y}{M_Y}<\varepsilon.
\]
Then for this fixed $Y$,
\[
\frac{|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|}{x}
\le
\varepsilon+\frac{R_Y}{x}.
\]
Letting $x\to\infty$ gives
\[
\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|}{x}\le \varepsilon.
\]
Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|=o(x)$.

Now if $S\subseteq\mathcal T$, then
\[
|A_S\cap[1,N]|=|\{s\in S:s^2\le N\}|=|S\cap[1,\sqrt N]|\le |\mathcal T\cap[1,\sqrt N]|=o(\sqrt N).
\]
This proves the obstruction claim.
\qed

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}

I now check the new work against the main failure modes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prime-power issue.}
The Round-1 argument only worked modulo $p$ for prime squares.
Lemma 12.3 fixes the exact missing point: it controls \emph{prime powers} $p^{2e}$, so Corollary 12.4 applies to arbitrary $u$ whose prime factors all lie in $3\pmod 4$, not just to primes.

\item \textbf{Necessity versus sufficiency in Theorem 12.5.}
Necessity is immediate from the residue-$0$ case of Round-1 Lemma 12.1: if $s$ divides two larger elements $t,u$, then $s^2\mid t^2+u^2$.
The nontrivial direction is sufficiency; this is exactly what Corollary 12.4 supplies.
So the criterion is genuinely biconditional.

\item \textbf{Distinctness of the semiprimes.}
In Proposition 12.7, $S_2$ is primitive because every element is squarefree with exactly two prime factors.
Thus divisibility between distinct elements is impossible by unique factorization; there is no hidden repeated-prime pathology.

\item \textbf{Double counting in Proposition 12.8.}
The lower bound counts semiprimes $pq$ by the smaller prime $p$ under the extra restriction $q>x^{1/3}$.
Since $p\le x^{1/3}<q$, each semiprime is counted exactly once.
So the lower bound is rigorous.

\item \textbf{Scope of the obstruction in Proposition 12.9.}
The conclusion $|A_S\cap[1,N]|=o(\sqrt N)$ is proved only for the restricted class $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$.
This is \emph{not} a disproof of Q1.
It is a disproof only of the natural Round-1 strategy class generated by the prime-square example.

\item \textbf{Interaction with Round-1 results.}
There is no contradiction with Lemma 12.2.
Prime squares correspond to the special case $S=\mathcal P_3$, where indeed each element divides no larger element.
Thus Theorem 12.5 strictly extends Round 1 rather than replacing it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{7) FINAL}

\textbf{UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED)}

The global questions Q1--Q3 remain open in this round, but the investigation has advanced in two mathematically substantive ways beyond Round 1.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exact structural reduction of the whole Round-1 square strategy.}
For sets of the form $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$, property $(*)$ is \emph{equivalent} to the purely multiplicative condition that each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$ (Theorem 12.5).
This is a complete characterization inside that natural class.

\item \textbf{A denser explicit family and a barrier.}
The semiprime-square family
\[
A_2=\{(pq)^2: p<q,\ p\equiv q\equiv 3\pmod 4\}
\]
satisfies $(*)$ and obeys
\[
|A_2\cap[1,N]|\gg \frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N},
\]
which is a strict improvement on the prime-square example from Round 1.
At the same time, Proposition 12.9 proves that \emph{every} construction in this entire $3\pmod 4$ square class has size $o(\sqrt N)$, so this strategy can never yield a positive answer to Q1.
\end{enumerate}

The sharp remaining gap is therefore clearer than after Round 1:
any eventual positive answer to Q1 must come from a construction outside the whole natural class
\[
A=\{s^2:s\in S,\ \text{all prime factors of $s$ are }3\pmod 4\},
\]
and any negative answer must rule out such completely different constructions.
