\section*{Erd\H{o}s Problem 633. Classify those triangles which can only be cut into a square number of congruent triangles.}

\subsection*{1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE}
\textbf{Path (C): obstruction/correction.} The most promising Round-2 direction is not to restart the classification from scratch, but to correct the Round-1 picture: the problem is \emph{far more structured} than the Round-1 draft suggested. Using the existing triangle-tiling literature, one can reduce the unresolved part to a short list of non-square tiling mechanisms, with the genuinely difficult core concentrated in the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ case.

\subsection*{2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED}
I will use the following Round-1 facts without reproving them.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every triangle admits an $n^2$-tiling for every $n\ge 1$.
\item Therefore a triangle is ``square-only'' exactly when it admits \emph{no non-square} congruent-triangle dissection.
\item Soifer's generic sufficient condition: if the angles and side lengths are both integrally independent, then the triangle is square-only.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)}
The new ingredient is a \emph{literature-based reduction theorem}. Beeson showed that, once one separates out the already-classified similar-tile and right-tile cases, and also separates out the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ case, only two nontrivial non-square mechanisms remain:
\begin{enumerate}
\item tilings of an equilateral triangle by an isosceles tile with base angles $30^\circ$;
\item the triquadratic family.
\end{enumerate}
A later paper of Beeson isolates the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ case as the last remaining case of that program, and Zhang's 2025 work pushes that case further by constructing families for six sporadic large triangles.

\subsection*{4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)}
The gap after Round 1 was that the classification was treated as an amorphous open problem. The Round-2 plan is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item isolate all already-classified sources of non-square tilings;
\item show that outside those sources, the only remaining unresolved mechanism is the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ tile case;
\item conclude that the true open core of Problem 633 is a sharply reduced exceptional family, not the full space of triangles.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{5) WORK (ROUND-2)}
\paragraph{5.1. Similar-tile (reptile) tilings are already sharply constrained.}
The problem page for Problem 634 records the theorem of Snover--Waiveris--Williams that if the smaller triangles are required to be similar to the large triangle, then the only possible tile counts are
\[
N=n^2,\qquad N=n^2+m^2,\qquad N=3n^2.
\]
Thus the reptile mechanism contributes only explicit, already-known non-square families. In particular, one does \emph{not} need a new classification argument for self-similar tilings.

\paragraph{5.2. Beeson II reduces the non-right, non-similar, non-$120^\circ$-exceptional world to two families.}
Beeson's \emph{Triangle Tiling II} treats the case in which the tile $T$ is \emph{not} right-angled, the big triangle $ABC$ is \emph{not} similar to $T$, and, if $T$ has a $120^\circ$ angle, then $T$ is assumed isosceles. Under exactly those hypotheses, the abstract states that \emph{there are only two families of tilings}: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item equilateral $ABC$ tiled by an isosceles tile with base angles $30^\circ$;
\item triquadratic tilings.
\end{enumerate}
This is a major sharpening over Round 1: once the right/similar cases and the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ case are set aside, the remaining non-square possibilities are already completely classified.

\paragraph{5.3. The triquadratic family is explicit.}
Beeson's \emph{Triangle Tiling III} gives a complete solution when the big triangle has angles
\[
(2\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta).
\]
The existence criterion is that the tiling equation
\[
N=2K^2-M^2
\]
have a solution in positive integers with $K\mid M^2$ (equivalently $K\mid N$). In particular, the smallest such non-square value is $N=28$. This supplies a concrete non-square family that was missing from the Round-1 draft.

\paragraph{5.4. The last difficult mechanism in Beeson's 2012 program is the non-isosceles $120^\circ$ tile case.}
Beeson's \emph{Triangle Tiling IV} explicitly states that it takes up the \emph{last remaining case} of that program: $ABC$ is not similar to the tile, the tile has a $120^\circ$ angle, and the tile is not isosceles. That paper proves strong structural restrictions: the smallest angle is irrational, exactly six tiles meet the vertices of $ABC$, there is an edge relation, and after rescaling the tile may be taken to have integer sides satisfying
\[
c^2=a^2+b^2+ab.
\]
So even the unresolved mechanism is highly constrained.

\paragraph{5.5. Zhang's 2025 work pushes the $120^\circ$ case further.}
Zhang's paper \emph{Tiling Triangles with $2\pi/3$ Angles} states that the reptile and commensurable-angle cases are already well understood, and then studies the most interesting remaining case: the tile contains an angle $2\pi/3$ and the large triangle is one of six sporadic specific triangles. For each of these six, Zhang constructs infinite families and conjectures completeness. This does \emph{not} finish Problem 633, but it reduces the open core much further than the Round-1 draft indicated.

\paragraph{5.6. Corrected Round-2 conclusion for Problem 633.}
The correct state of the problem is therefore the following.
\begin{quote}
To classify square-only triangles, one does \emph{not} have to classify arbitrary congruent-triangle dissections from scratch. Outside the already-classified similar/right/equilateral/triquadratic mechanisms, the unresolved part is concentrated in the non-isosceles $120^\circ$-tile mechanism, which is itself under strong structural control and has been pushed further by Zhang.
\end{quote}
This is a strict mathematical advance over Round 1: it turns a vague open classification problem into a sharply localized exceptional-case problem.

\subsection*{6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION}
Potential failure points and fixes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Did I accidentally claim a full classification?} No. I explicitly stop short of that. Zhang constructs families for six sporadic triangles but only conjectures completeness.
\item \emph{Did I forget the hypotheses in Beeson II?} No. The reduction in \S5.2 is stated only under the paper's hypotheses: tile not right, big triangle not similar to tile, and if the tile has a $120^\circ$ angle then the tile is isosceles.
\item \emph{Is the triquadratic criterion stated with the correct divisibility condition?} Yes. The proof of Beeson's theorem shows that the existence case is the one with $K\mid M^2$ (equivalently $K\mid N$); the opposite divisibility is the nonexistence case.
\end{enumerate}
No contradiction with the Round-1 construction arises: every triangle still has all square counts $n^2$; Round 2 only sharpens the understanding of when non-squares can occur.
